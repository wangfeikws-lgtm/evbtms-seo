# Selection Guide for High-Voltage PTC Heaters for Electric Vehicles

The choice of a high-voltage PTC heater should be based on the heating function it is required to perform, rather than on its rated power in kilowatts. Determine what the battery system requires — preheating, cabin heating or both — under real-world operating conditions, and then tailor the heater's voltage platform, power range, control interface and coolant loop to that function. Ensure that these four aspects are agreed in writing before approving the samples; this will help you avoid most integration issues.

A high-voltage coolant heater (HVCH) is a PTC heater that heats the coolant for battery pre-heating and cabin heating in electric buses, lorries, mining lorries and construction machinery. Our range covers power ratings of 6–18 kW, 20–25 kW and 30–35 kW on 400 V, 600 V and 800 V platforms, with bespoke power ratings available on request. This guide outlines the same checks we ask buyers to carry out before quoting for a configuration.

## Define the heating task before determining the rated power

First, check with the battery supplier regarding the applicable temperature range and operating limits. Clarify the intended use: battery pre-heating, cabin heating or a combination of both. Note down the battery's initial state, the ambient conditions, the time available for heating and the vehicle's operating mode. The power available whilst the vehicle is parked and charging may differ from that available whilst it is in motion; therefore, the definition of the task must cover both scenarios.

Never calculate the heating time based solely on the rated power stated on the nameplate. The battery mass, initial temperature, heat loss, coolant distribution and available electrical power all influence the final result. Heating objectives and test boundary conditions must be agreed jointly with the battery team and the vehicle integration team before ordering any samples.

## Match the voltage and power at the same operating point

Obtain the minimum and maximum vehicle supply voltages for each relevant operating mode. Compare these values with the operating limits of the selected heater configuration and do not rely solely on the platform's rated specifications, such as 400 V or 800 V.

Ask suppliers to specify the voltage, coolant inlet temperature and flow rate conditions under which the stated heat output was measured. Our heaters are supplied with a power output of between 15 and 35 kW per unit, with platform voltages of up to 870 V DC, and have been in series production since 2021. For reference, 400 V and 800 V are the standard reference values for electric vehicle platforms, and Webasto states that its HVH 50/70 units have a power output of between 5 and 7 kW. The power band and platform voltage are a single decision factor, not two: a heater that fits the voltage range but cannot provide the necessary heat in the coldest start-up conditions is an unsuitable heater, however impressive its technical specifications may seem.

System integrators must also verify the electrical power budget. Where other vehicle loads share the same power supply, the rated power stated on the type plate does not represent the power actually available to the heating system at any given time. The selection of cables, connectors and protective devices must comply with the vehicle's approved electrical design.

## Check the control interface: CAN or thermostat

The high-voltage power supply for the heater and the low-voltage power supply for the control system must be kept separate. Obtain the connector diagrams, power supply requirements and interface information for the selected model. Some configurations use CAN communication; others use a thermostat. Do not assume that all heaters use the same interface.

Our B series uses a CAN control system with an activation signal; our E series uses a thermostat and does not have CAN. Please clarify who issues the heating request, which signals confirm the actual operating status, which controller provides information on operating limits, and how the vehicle should respond in the event of a communication failure. Sharing a CAN bus does not constitute a complete control strategy. Please confirm in writing the signal definitions, software versions and diagnostic procedures for the proposed project.

## Check the installation of the coolant loop

A heater is a resistive element in a coolant loop, which may also contain a water pump, hoses, valves, cold plates and other heat exchangers. Ask the supplier for the pressure drop data for this heater for the specific coolant type, temperature and flow conditions. Compare the requirements of the entire circuit with the pump's performance curve. Where the circuit contains parallel branches, the total flow rate of the pump will differ from the flow rate through the heater branch.

Clarify how the flow rate is measured through the selected branch in each operating mode. Under cold start conditions, consult the pump and heater suppliers to confirm which data apply to the selected coolant and the initial temperature conditions.

Include the agreed items in a single record to be signed by both the system supplier and the heater supplier. Mark any missing parameters as 'to be determined'; suppliers cannot validate a complete system configuration based on an incomplete list of assumptions.

## Validate the samples in the installed system

Before commencing the tests, the teams involved must agree on the initial condition of the battery and the coolant, the location of the sensors, the operating modes and the acceptance criteria. A standard time reference must be adopted when recording the load requests and the heaters' response, power conditions, coolant inlet and outlet temperatures, and flow rates in the heater circuit. Steady-state heating and the transition between the different operating modes must be evaluated.

When measured data deviates from expectations, retain the raw data and verify the interfaces and test limits in conjunction with the relevant suppliers. Never bypass safety features in order to obtain ideal results; fault injection tests must be carried out in accordance with authorised test procedures. Our Q-series heaters have documented laboratory validation studies covering vibration assessment, water circulation and operational performance, carried out at Shanghai University of Engineering Science. Validating samples in your installed system remains important: the laboratory results describe the heater, whilst your vehicle describes the installation.

## Confirm the production configuration

Once the sample has been accepted, document the confirmed reference numbers, drawing versions, connector layout, applicable control interfaces and any customer-specific options. System manufacturers and heater suppliers must agree on a mechanism for communicating changes and clarify which changes require further technical review. Demand forecasts and expected lead times may serve as a reference for procurement. For specific orders, actual production capacity, lead times and commercial terms must be confirmed. Approval of a sample does not imply that other versions of hardware or software are equivalent.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is a high-voltage PTC heater?** A high-voltage PTC heater is an electric heater that heats the coolant or air in an electric vehicle, using a positive temperature coefficient element whose resistance increases as it heats up. In electric vehicle applications, it is typically used for battery preheating and cabin heating, and operates on the vehicle's high-voltage platform rather than on the 12 V or 24 V system.

**How does a coolant heater differ from an air heater?** A coolant heater heats the fluid in a coolant loop that transfers heat to the battery or the passenger compartment heat exchanger. An air heater heats the air directly. Coolant heaters are suitable for battery thermal management, as the same circuit serves the battery, whilst air heaters are suitable for use exclusively in the passenger compartment. The selection criteria differ: coolant heaters require verification of pressure drop and flow rate, whilst air heaters require verification of air flow.

**Which voltage platform do I need?** Match the heater to your vehicle's actual supply voltages in all operating modes, not to the nominal values stated on the label. Our range covers 400 V, 600 V and 800 V platforms with customised ranges, with power outputs from 15 to 35 kW per unit and platform voltages of up to 870 V DC. Ask the supplier to specify the conditions under which the rated power was measured.

**CAN control or thermostat control?** It depends on your vehicle's architecture. CAN control with an activation signal is suitable for vehicles that manage the heating via a central controller. Thermostat control is suitable for simpler installations that do not have a CAN node for the heating system. Before making your choice, check who issues the heating request and how the vehicle responds in the event of a communication fault.

**What should I ask the supplier before approving the samples?** Request the definition of the heating task, the voltage and power at the same operating point, pressure drop data for your coolant and temperatures, documentation on connectors and interfaces, and the process for communicating changes. Agree on the test conditions and acceptance criteria before testing begins, not after the results prove disappointing.

**Do you supply a complete heating system or just the heater?** We supply the heater and provide support for its integration with your vehicle's equipment: configuration selection, interface documentation, and sample validation. System-level components, such as pumps, hoses, and cold plates, are specified jointly for your project. Please send us the details of your application to receive a written quote.
