<p>Start with the failure, because that is where heating systems cost money. A heater quits in January and the vehicle stays in the yard; nobody cares which unit was cheaper on the purchase order. Both architectures suffer it, and neither camp's brochure will tell you otherwise. We manufacture coolant heaters, so discount what follows for that bias — then check the arithmetic anyway, because the quote is the smallest part of this one.</p>

<h2>On price, air wins, and it is not close</h2>

<p>An air PTC heater is a heating element, a fan and a housing — not much to it, and that is a compliment. A coolant heater is a pressure-rated liquid device: high-voltage connectors, seals, a control board, and it expects a pump and plumbing around it. Unit for unit air is cheaper to buy and to ship; any supplier arguing otherwise is selling something. Air has that line.</p>

<h2>Installation turns on one question: is there a loop?</h2>

<p>Air heat needs ducting and high-voltage wiring run to where the people sit. Coolant heat needs hoses, fittings, a bleed procedure and a place in the liquid loop. Quotes price the two lists as equivalent work. On a modern commercial EV they are not: the battery is liquid-cooled, so the loop is already there — circuit routed, pump fitted, coolant already on the service schedule. Tapping a heater into that circuit is incremental, not a new system.</p>

<p>Reverse the vehicle and the answer reverses. On an older or air-cooled platform with no loop at all, a coolant heater drags a whole circuit in behind it — pump, hoses, reservoir, the lot — and the air heater wins installation outright. We would tell the buyer so.</p>

<h2>The battery is the argument</h2>

<p>An air heater heats air. It cannot warm a liquid-cooled battery. That is not a limitation to engineer around; it is what the device is. In a cold climate the battery needs heat regardless — for charge acceptance, for power, for longevity. The northern fleet that bought the cheaper heater then buys battery heating as a second, separate system: a second control integration, and a second way for the vehicle to end up in the yard.</p>

<p>One coolant heater does both jobs through one loop and a set of valves. It warms the pack before departure, then serves the cabin on the road. That is why coolant heaters dominate commercial EVs despite the higher unit price — not that the heater is cheaper, but that the second heater never gets bought.</p>

<h2>Energy, honestly, is a draw</h2>

<p>Resistive heating turns electricity into heat at roughly one to one, whichever box it happens in. Twenty kilowatts for one hour is twenty kilowatt-hours, out of either box. Anyone claiming a dramatic efficiency gap between the two is marketing at you.</p>

<p>The differences sit at the edges. A coolant system can precondition pack and cabin on depot power before departure, spending grid energy instead of battery energy, and on the road it can blend motor waste heat into the same loop. An air heater warms a cabin quickly and hands the heat back as quickly when the doors open and the fan stops — on a city bus duty cycle you can see it in the energy log. A few percent. Not a revolution.</p>

<h2>What the fleet lives with afterwards</h2>

<p>Maintenance, first. A coolant heater rides on a circuit the fleet already maintains; coolant changes, hose inspections and pump life are known quantities. Air heater elements live in dusty ducts and take a thermal shock at every cold start. Neither is maintenance-free, whatever the brochures say.</p>

<p>When a heater does fail in January, the vehicle is out of service under both architectures; what differs is what happens next. One heating system type across the fleet means one spare on the shelf and one diagnostic routine every technician knows. Fleets that split architectures to save on the purchase price hand the saving back in spares and training within a few winters.</p>

<p>Drivers, last, because no spreadsheet carries them. A cab that warms slowly on a January morning produces complaints, workarounds, and eventually a request to idle something that should not be idling. A vehicle that preconditioned on the plug leaves warm and generates no emails.</p>

<h2>Where we land</h2>

<p>Choose air when the vehicle has no liquid loop, the climate is mild, the job is cabin-only, or the project is a retrofit where plumbing is impractical. Small vans and spot heating for a sleeper cab are fair examples, and on those vehicles our own product is the wrong buy. Choose coolant when the battery is liquid-cooled, winters are real, or the vehicle has to precondition on shore power — which covers most commercial EV programmes we see. Run your duty cycle through the costs above before either quote on price.</p>

<h2>Questions we hear on this topic</h2>

<h3>Is a coolant heater worth it on a small electric van?</h3>
<p>Depends on the battery and the winters. Liquid-cooled pack, cold climate: usually yes, because the battery-heating job is already there waiting. Air-cooled pack, southern duty: the air heater is the rational buy, and we would say so.</p>

<h3>Does a coolant heater warm the cabin more slowly?</h3>
<p>Slightly, on a fully cold start — the loop has to come up to temperature first. The difference is minutes, and preconditioning while plugged in removes it entirely.</p>

<h3>Should we compare either option against a heat pump?</h3>
<p>On any new platform, yes. Heat pumps cut heating energy in moderate cold and lose ground in deep cold, which is why so many vehicles pair one with a PTC heater. We compared the two in our heat pump article.</p>

<p><strong>Costing a heating architecture?</strong> Send us the vehicle, the climate, the battery cooling type and the duty cycle. We will work through the costs with you, including when the answer is air.</p>
