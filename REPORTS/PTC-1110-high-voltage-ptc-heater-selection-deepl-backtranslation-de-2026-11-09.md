# Guide to Selecting High-Voltage PTC Heaters for Electric Vehicles

Selecting a high-voltage PTC heater starts with the heating task, not with the rated power in kilowatts. Determine what the battery system requires under real-world operating conditions—preheating, cabin heating, or both—and then tailor the heater's voltage platform, power range, control interface, and cooling circuit to that task. Document these four matches in writing before approving samples—this will prevent most integration issues from arising in the first place.

A high-voltage coolant heater (HVCH) is a PTC heater that heats the coolant for battery preheating and cabin heating in electric buses, trucks, mining trucks, and construction machinery. Our product line covers power ranges of 6–18 kW, 20–25 kW, and 30–35 kW on 400-V, 600-V, and 800-V platforms, with custom ranges on request. This guide walks you through the same checks we require from buyers before we submit a quote for a configuration.

## Determine the heating requirement before specifying the rated power

First, check with the battery supplier regarding the permissible temperature range and operating limits. Clarify the intended use: battery pre-heating, cabin heating or a combination of both. Record the battery's initial state, the ambient conditions, the available heating time and the vehicle's operating mode. The available power budget whilst parked and charging may differ from that whilst driving, so the specification must cover both scenarios.

Never estimate the heating time based solely on the rated power specified on the type plate. Battery mass, initial temperature, heat loss, coolant distribution and available electrical power all influence the final result. Heating targets and test boundary conditions should be agreed in consultation with the battery team and the vehicle integration team before samples are ordered.

## Match voltage and power at the same operating point

Determine the minimum and maximum supply voltages for the vehicle in each relevant operating mode. Compare these values with the operating limits of the selected heater configuration and do not rely solely on the nominal values specified on the platform, such as 400 V or 800 V. Ask suppliers to specify the conditions regarding voltage, coolant inlet temperature and flow rate under which their stated heating output was measured.

Our heaters are available with an output of 15–35 kW per unit and platform voltages of up to 870 V DC, and have been in series production since 2021. By way of comparison: 400 V and 800 V are the standard reference values for electric vehicle platforms, and Webasto specifies an output of 5–7 kW for its HVH 50/70 units. Power range and voltage platform are one decision, not two: a heater that, whilst fitting within the voltage range, cannot deliver the required heat under the coldest start-up conditions is the wrong heater, no matter how good its technical specifications look.

System integrators must also check the electrical power budget. If other vehicle components share the same power supply, the rated power stated on the type plate does not correspond to the power actually available for the heater at any given time. The selection of cables, connectors and protective devices must be consistent with the approved electrical vehicle design.

## Check the control interface: CAN or thermostat

The high-voltage heating power and the low-voltage control voltage should remain separate from one another. Obtain connector diagrams, power requirements and interface information for the selected model. Some configurations use CAN communication, whilst others use a thermostat. Do not assume that all heaters use the same interface.

Our B-Series uses CAN control with an enable signal; our E-Series uses a thermostat and does not have CAN. Clarify who triggers the heating request, which signals confirm the actual operating status, which controller provides feedback on operating limits, and how the vehicle should respond in the event of a communication failure. Simply sharing a CAN bus does not constitute a complete control strategy. Confirm signal definitions, software versions and diagnostic procedures in writing for the planned project.

## Check the installation of the coolant circuit

A heater is a resistive element within a cooling circuit, which may also contain a water pump, hoses, valves, cold plates and other heat exchangers. Request pressure drop data for this heater from the supplier under the specific conditions relating to coolant type, temperature and flow rate. Compare the requirements of the entire circuit with the pump's performance curve.

If the circuit contains parallel branches, the pump's total flow rate will differ from the flow rate in the heater branch. Clarify how the flow rate through the selected branch is measured in each operating mode. For cold start conditions, contact the pump and heater suppliers to confirm which data applies to the selected coolant and the initial temperature conditions.

Summarise the agreed points in a single document, to be signed by both the system supplier and the heater supplier. Mark any missing parameters as 'to be determined'; suppliers cannot validate a complete system configuration on the basis of an incomplete list of assumptions.

## Validation of samples in the installed system

Before testing begins, the teams responsible should agree on the initial state of the battery and coolant, the sensor positions, the operating modes and the acceptance criteria. Use a standardised time reference when recording heating requirements and feedback, power conditions, coolant inlet and outlet temperatures, and heater branch flow rates.

Evaluate steady-state heating operation and the transition between operating modes. If the measured data deviates from expectations, the raw data should be retained and the interfaces and test limits reviewed in consultation with the relevant suppliers. Safety functions must never be bypassed to achieve ideal results; the induction of faults is only permitted within the framework of approved test procedures.

Documented laboratory validations are available for our Q-series heaters, comprising vibration, water circulation and performance tests carried out at Shanghai University of Engineering Science. However, it is still important to validate samples within your installed system: the laboratory results describe the heater, whilst your vehicle reflects the installed condition.

## Confirmation of the production configuration

Following acceptance of the sample, the confirmed part numbers, drawing versions, connector layouts, applicable control interfaces and any customer-specific options must be documented. System manufacturers and heater suppliers should agree on a mechanism for communicating changes and clarify which changes require further technical review.

Demand forecasts and target delivery dates can serve as a guide for procurement. For specific orders, the actual production capacity, lead times and terms and conditions must be confirmed. The approval of a sample does not imply that other hardware or software versions are equivalent.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is a high-voltage PTC heater?** A high-voltage PTC heater is an electric heater that heats coolant or air in an electric vehicle. It utilises an element with a positive temperature coefficient, whose resistance increases when heated. In electric vehicles, it is generally used for battery pre-heating and cabin heating, and is powered via the vehicle's high-voltage platform rather than the 12 V or 24 V system.

**How does a coolant heater differ from an air heater?** A coolant heater heats the fluid in a cooling circuit that transfers heat to the battery or cabin heat exchanger. An air heater heats the air directly. Coolant heaters are suitable for battery thermal management, as the same circuit supplies the battery, whilst air heaters are suitable exclusively for cabin heating applications. The selection criteria differ: for coolant heaters, pressure drop and flow rate must be checked; for air heaters, the air flow must be checked.

**Which voltage platform do I need?** Match the heater to your vehicle's actual supply voltages in the various operating modes, not to the rated values on the type plate. Our range includes 400 V, 600 V and 800 V platforms with customisable power ranges of 15–35 kW per unit and platform voltages of up to 870 V DC. Ask the supplier to specify the conditions under which the rated power was measured.

**CAN or thermostat control – which is the right choice?** That depends on your vehicle architecture. CAN control with an enable signal is suitable for vehicles where the heater is regulated via a central control unit. Thermostat control is suitable for simpler installations without a CAN node for the heater. Before making your selection, clarify who triggers the heating request and how the vehicle responds to a communication failure.

**What should I ask the supplier before approving samples?** Ask for the definition of the heating task, the voltage and power at the same operating point, the pressure drop data for your coolant and the temperatures, the documentation on connectors and interfaces, and the procedure for communicating changes. Agree on the test conditions and acceptance criteria before testing begins, rather than waiting until the results prove disappointing.

**Do you supply a complete heating system or just the heater?** We supply the heater and support its integration in collaboration with your vehicle team: configuration selection, interface documentation and sample validation. System-level components such as pumps, hoses and cold plates are specified jointly for your project. Please send us your application details for a written quotation.
