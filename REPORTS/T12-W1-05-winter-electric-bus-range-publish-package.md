## HTML body

```html
<p>There is no universal “40% winter range loss” for every electric bus. Published results vary with vehicle, route, temperature, battery, heating architecture and operating practice. The useful question for a fleet manager is not whether one dramatic number is true. It is which parts of winter energy use the fleet can measure and control.</p>

<h2>Why winter range is not one number</h2>
<p>Cold conditions affect several systems at once. The battery may deliver less usable energy, while the vehicle consumes additional power to warm the battery, cabin and windscreen. Frequent door opening replaces heated cabin air with cold outside air. Cold-soaked coolant, structure and interior materials add a large start-of-day load.</p>

<p>Route speed, passenger load, topography, idle time and charging opportunities further change the result. A percentage observed on one fleet should not be copied into another fleet’s plan without the original conditions.</p>

<h2>Lever 1: precondition while connected</h2>
<p>Where the vehicle and charger support it, warm the battery and cabin before departure while connected to depot power. This does not remove the heating load, but it can shift part of that energy away from the route battery.</p>

<h2>Lever 2: separate battery, cabin and defrost loads</h2>
<p>A fleet needs visibility into where the energy goes. Battery conditioning protects operation and charging performance. Cabin heating and defrost support safety and passenger service. Treating them as one uncontrolled load makes diagnosis and sizing difficult.</p>

<h2>Lever 3: size for the cold duty case</h2>
<p>Average winter temperature is not enough. Define cold-soak temperature, departure time, cabin occupancy, door-opening frequency, battery target, route duration and available charging power. Then determine the thermal capacity and control strategy required for the worst duty case the fleet intends to serve.</p>

<p>A high-voltage coolant heater can be part of that strategy. EVLINK’s Q Series covers 15–35 kW at 400–1100 V with CAN plus enable control. It is a candidate heat source, not a promise to restore a fixed percentage of range.</p>

<h2>Lever 4: protect operational reserve</h2>
<p>Winter schedules may need a larger state-of-charge reserve, opportunity charging, longer layovers or spare vehicles for extreme days. The correct reserve should be based on route data rather than the nominal vehicle range.</p>

<h2>Lever 5: measure each route</h2>
<p>Record ambient temperature, traction energy, HVAC energy, battery-conditioning energy, idle time, charging, passenger load and route completion. Compare similar routes and temperatures. After several cold weeks, the fleet can replace assumptions with its own operating curve.</p>

<h2>What an HVCH can—and cannot—solve</h2>
<p>A properly selected HVCH can provide controlled heat to a battery or cabin coolant loop. It cannot compensate for an undersized battery, poor body insulation, inadequate depot power or an unrealistic timetable. Those are vehicle and fleet-system questions.</p>

<h2>Data to include in an HVCH RFQ</h2>
<ul>
<li>Vehicle and route type</li>
<li>HV minimum, nominal and maximum voltage</li>
<li>Battery and cabin heat-load targets</li>
<li>Cold-soak and operating-temperature range</li>
<li>Coolant, flow and pressure conditions</li>
<li>Control, diagnostic and fault requirements</li>
<li>Warm-up, energy and vehicle validation plan</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Turn winter data into a heater requirement.</strong> Send EVLINK one representative cold-week route dataset together with the vehicle electrical and coolant-loop inputs. We can help identify the heater requirement and the tests needed before deployment.</p>
```
