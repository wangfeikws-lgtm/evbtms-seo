<h2>Why orientation belongs on the drawing, not on the install day</h2>
<p>Every installer knows the pattern. The loop is filled, the covers are on, and the first heat request comes back as noise in the pipes, uneven heating, and a heater that cuts out on overtemperature. The team drains the loop, checks the wiring, checks the pump. The cause sits higher up the page: the heater ended up at the highest point of the loop, and the only vent sits behind a bracket nobody can reach with the covers fitted. By then the bracket is welded, the hoses are cut to length, and the fix is rework, not adjustment.</p>
<p>We write this guide for the meeting where that outcome is still cheap to prevent: the drawing review. The questions below apply whether the unit on the drawing is a 20–24 kW E series unit or a larger unit. Only the answers change, and they come from that model's approved drawing, not from a generic table.</p>

<h2>The drawing review checklist</h2>
<p>Work through these seven items on the drawing itself, before anyone approves it. An item is not closed because someone says it will be fine on the vehicle. It is closed when the drawing shows it, or when a named document states it.</p>

<h3>1. The allowed orientation envelope for the exact model</h3>
<p>The drawing for the exact model must state which orientations are allowed and which are forbidden. Some units tolerate a range of mounting angles. Others do not, because the internal layout traps air in one position and clears it in another. Never accept "any orientation works" as an answer. If the envelope is not marked on the drawing, the review is not finished, and the bracket design cannot start.</p>

<h3>2. Inlet, outlet, and flow direction</h3>
<p>Check that the inlet and outlet ports are marked on the drawing and that the flow direction matches the loop: pump, then heater, then load. Reversed ports do more than reduce performance. They leave air sitting at the top of the unit, where no normal circulation can push it out. The drawing should make the correct connection obvious to the person holding the hose, not something to work out from memory.</p>

<h3>3. Where is the high point, and where does air go?</h3>
<p>Find the highest point of the whole loop on the drawing, not just the heater. As a rule, the expansion tank should sit above the heater so air has somewhere to go. If the heater itself is the highest point, it needs its own vent path, and that path must be drawn, not assumed. Ask where air collects at first fill, and again after a coolant change, because those are the two moments when air enters the system.</p>

<h3>4. Can anyone reach the vent after installation?</h3>
<p>A vent that exists only on paper is not a vent. With the unit installed, the covers fitted, and the neighboring hardware in place, can a technician reach the bleed point, open it, and see coolant rather than just hear air? If reaching it means removing the unit first, the drawing fails this item. Vent access is a service question, and service questions belong on the drawing review, not on the workshop floor.</p>

<h3>5. Who owns the fill and vent procedure?</h3>
<p>The review must name who writes the fill and vent procedure, who signs it, and what equipment it assumes. A procedure written for a vacuum fill rig does not help a workshop that fills by gravity, and a procedure that covers first fill but not post-maintenance fill leaves half the problem open. If no owner is named, the procedure is an open item, and open items should block approval.</p>

<h3>6. Service space and hose loads</h3>
<p>Can the unit come out without draining the entire loop or removing the part next to it? Are the hoses routed without kinks, pitched so air can travel toward the vent, and supported so the weight of the unit does not hang on the ports? Are the clamps tightened to the torque value stated on the drawing? That last point matters: torque values belong to a specific model and a specific joint. Never borrow them from another model's table, and never let a fitter guess them.</p>

<h3>7. Prototype record and drawing change control</h3>
<p>At first fit, photograph the installed orientation against the drawing, record the venting result, and file both under the drawing number. If the fit differs from the drawing, the drawing gets revised. Silent deviations are how the next vehicle inherits the same fault, because the next team builds to the same uncorrected page.</p>

<h2>What trapped air actually does</h2>
<p>Air inside the heater leaves part of the element dry. That part runs hot, the protection trips, and heating stops, which looks like a heater fault but is a loop fault. Air at the pump inlet makes the pump lose prime and turn noisy. Air moving around the loop gives gurgling, uneven heating, and fault codes that send the next technician looking at sensors and wiring. None of this needs a failed component. It needs a pocket of air in the wrong place, and the drawing review is where that pocket gets designed out.</p>

<h2>Red flags that stop approval</h2>
<ul>
<li>The orientation envelope is missing from the drawing.</li>
<li>The heater sits at the loop high point with no vent path drawn.</li>
<li>A vent exists but cannot be reached after installation.</li>
<li>The fill procedure assumes equipment the workshop does not have.</li>
<li>Hoses are kinked, carry the unit's weight, or are clamped to a borrowed torque value.</li>
<li>The prototype fit deviates from the drawing with no change note.</li>
<li>The control variant on the drawing does not match the ordered variant.</li>
</ul>

<h2>What to ask the supplier in writing</h2>
<p>Send these questions with the drawing package, and keep the answers with the order. This guide states none of these values, because every one of them belongs to a specific model drawing:</p>
<ul>
<li>Orientation envelope for the exact model, marked on the drawing.</li>
<li>Port positions and flow direction.</li>
<li>Vent location, vent path, and access with the unit installed.</li>
<li>Fill and vent procedure document, with number, revision, and the equipment it assumes.</li>
<li>Service clearances and the removal path.</li>
<li>Hose support points, bend limits, and clamp torque values from the drawing.</li>
<li>Weight and mounting interface data for the bracket design.</li>
</ul>

<h2>What to send us</h2>
<p>If you want us to confirm a layout before approval, send the installation drawing, or the loop layout with the heater position marked, plus the fill equipment available in your workshop and the control variant ordered. We check those against the model drawing and flag conflicts before approval, while they are still comments on a page. We do not confirm layouts for models we have not drawn, and we do not guess values for them.</p>

<h2>Frequently asked questions</h2>
<h3>Can the heater be mounted in any orientation?</h3>
<p>No. Only the orientations stated on the approved drawing for that exact model. A unit that tolerates one position can trap air in another, and the drawing is the only document that says which is which.</p>
<h3>Does every loop need a separate vent?</h3>
<p>Not necessarily. It depends on where the loop's high point is and whether air can reach the expansion tank on its own. The drawing review must answer this for the specific layout. Assuming it either way is how air pockets survive to commissioning.</p>
<h3>Who owns the fill and vent procedure?</h3>
<p>Whoever the review names, in writing, before approval: supplier or integrator. An unnamed procedure is an open item, not a plan, and it should block approval until it has an owner and a document number.</p>
<h3>What if the prototype fit differs from the drawing?</h3>
<p>Stop, photograph it, record it, and revise the drawing. Do not carry a silent deviation into production. The next vehicle will be built to the same page, and it will inherit the same fault.</p>
