# Where Does Regenerative Braking Energy Go When the Battery Is Full?

Ask where regenerative braking energy goes when the battery is full, and most answers stop at one sentence: the friction brakes take over. For a passenger car rolling down a hill for ninety seconds, that is good enough. For a loaded truck at the top of a twenty-minute descent, it is where the real problem starts.

Energy does not disappear because the battery stopped accepting it. A descending truck keeps turning height into electricity whether the pack wants it or not, and every kilowatt lands somewhere: in the battery, in the brake discs as heat, or in a resistor. On a heavy vehicle, that third path has to be designed and cooled on purpose. Leave it to chance and the descent ends with faded brakes.

## The battery says no more often than you think

Three ordinary conditions cut the pack's charge acceptance.

The first is a high state of charge. Near full, the BMS tapers charge current hard to protect the cells, and at 100% acceptance is effectively zero. The regen curve on a datasheet is drawn with a half-empty pack. The top of a hill after a full charge is a different vehicle.

The second is cold. A pack at deep-winter temperatures accepts only a fraction of its warm charge rate, so regen weakens on a cold morning at 60% charge.

The third is the descent itself: a truck that starts down at 85% can hit the BMS ceiling halfway down, because the descent is refilling the pack as it goes. Regen fades where the grade still has kilometres to run, and on bad routes all three stack.

## Run the descent math before blaming the brakes

Take a loaded truck at 60 tonnes gross, descending 300 vertical metres. The potential energy alone is about 177 MJ, roughly 49 kWh. Losses take their share, but the motor will still hand the DC bus some 40 kWh in that one descent. Over twenty minutes, that is a sustained average load well above 100 kW, with no pauses.

Hold that against the battery's side of the ledger. If the pack arrives at the top full, cold, or both, its share of that 40 kWh is close to zero. The energy does not shrink to match. Whatever the pack refuses must still be absorbed on the vehicle, at the same rate, for the same twenty minutes.

This is the arithmetic we size against. One hard descent, one full resistor event. The rating came from descent profiles like this one, not motor nameplates.

## Friction brakes are sprinters, not distance runners

Brake discs are built for one duty: very high power, for seconds, followed by a long rest. A service stop from highway speed is exactly that; a twenty-minute drag down a grade is the opposite duty, continuous moderate power with no rest.

Under continuous drag, disc and pad temperatures climb past the point where the friction material behaves. The pedal goes long, stopping distances grow, and the wear bill arrives early. Drivers call it fade. No pad compound fixes it, because the problem is not friction. It is heat rejection: a disc sheds heat to the air around the wheel, and on a slow descent that airflow is weakest exactly when the heat input lasts longest.

So "the friction brakes take over" on a long grade means converting 40 kWh into heat at four wheel ends sized for stops. They remain the final safety layer. They are simply the wrong tool for continuous energy disposal. We covered the same duty mismatch from the sizing side in [how duty cycle determines braking resistor size](https://evbtms.com/duty-cycle-braking-resistor-sizing/).

## The resistor is the planned overflow path

A braking resistor across the DC bus gives the refused energy a controlled exit. When the inverter cannot push current into the pack, a switch meters current through the resistor, and the energy leaves as heat in the coolant loop rather than heat in the brake discs. How the resistor itself works is covered in [how braking resistors work in electric vehicles](https://evbtms.com/how-braking-resistors-work-electric-vehicles/); the point here is the destination, not the mechanism.

Liquid cooling is what makes the path continuous. The HVH-R1402 is a 2.24 Ω unit (±10%, cold at 25 °C) for 450–850 V DC buses, sealed to IP6K9K and rated from −40 to +85 °C. Its DN32 coolant ports move the heat into the vehicle cooling circuit at 172 L/min or more, where a radiator rejects it for as long as the descent lasts. An NTC sensor watches its temperature, and the high-voltage interlock confirms the connectors are seated.

Bus voltage sets the ceiling on that path, because power through a fixed resistor follows P = U²/R. Fully on across a 450 V bus, the same 2.24 Ω unit delivers about 90 kW. At 850 V it delivers about 323 kW, metered down by switching to whatever the descent actually demands. Same resistor, very different headroom. This is why the voltage window is one of the first things we ask for.

## FAQ

**Does regen only disappear at exactly 100% charge?**
No. Charge acceptance tapers well before full, so a pack at 92% on a cold morning may accept less regen than a warm pack at 70%. Plan for the taper, not the gauge.

**If the energy ends up as heat anyway, was regen pointless?**
No. The resistor only takes the overflow the battery refused. What the pack could accept was still recovered and reused; the resistor handles the remainder that would otherwise have gone into the brake discs, uncontrolled, wearing them out.

**Can the resistor hold the truck on the grade by itself?**
It takes the continuous drag load, the part that destroys friction brakes. Friction brakes still handle full stops, holding at standstill and backup. The split is by duration: seconds belong to the discs, minutes to the resistor.
