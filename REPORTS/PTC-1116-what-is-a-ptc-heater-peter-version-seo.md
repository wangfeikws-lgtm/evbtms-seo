# What Is a PTC Heater? How It Heats EVs Without an Engine

PTC heaters offer the simplest solution to a common problem faced by electric vehicles: as they have no engine, they cannot utilise waste heat to warm the passenger compartment or the battery. PTC heaters directly convert electrical energy from the power battery into heat, and are self-regulating in the process. This self-regulating capability is at the very heart of their design and distinguishes PTC heaters from conventional resistance wire heaters.

## The true meaning of PTC

PTC stands for ‘Positive Temperature Coefficient’. Its heating element is made of a ceramic material (usually barium titanate), the resistance of which increases as the temperature rises. When an electric current flows through the element, heat is generated.

As the element’s temperature rises, its resistance increases, the current decreases accordingly, and the heat output drops as a result. When the temperature exceeds a certain threshold, the resistance rises sharply, enabling the element to automatically limit its own heat generation.

This is crucial, as its safety stems from the inherent properties of the material itself, rather than relying on control circuits. Thermostats may fail, and controllers may lose signal, but the physical properties of the PTC element remain unchanged: the higher the temperature, the greater the resistance; the greater the resistance, the lower the current.

For this very reason, it is highly unlikely that a PTC heater will overheat, even if the vehicle’s control system fails. Whilst it still needs to work in conjunction with the vehicle’s own protection systems (we would never market it as a complete heating solution based solely on the material’s properties), this self-limiting characteristic does indeed provide a second layer of safety that ordinary resistive heaters lack.

## How does it heat an electric vehicle without an engine?

In petrol and diesel vehicles, cabin heat is virtually ‘free’—the engine converts most of the fuel’s energy into waste heat whilst running, and the cooling system transfers a portion of this heat to the cabin. Electric vehicles, however, lack this source of ‘waste heat’.

Electric vehicle drive motors are extremely efficient and cannot generate sufficient excess heat; furthermore, the vehicles have no exhaust system. Consequently, the vehicle must actively draw energy from the battery to generate heat, and the PTC heater is the most direct way to achieve this: it takes high-voltage direct current as input and outputs heat, without the need for any moving parts.

It should be clearly noted that this directness comes at a cost. PTC heaters convert electrical energy into thermal energy, with a coefficient of performance (COP) of at most 1:1—meaning that one unit of battery energy can be converted into at most one unit of thermal energy, and never more.

Heat pumps perform better because they do not ‘generate’ heat, but rather ‘transfer’ it. The advantages of PTC heaters lie in their low cost, compact size and rapid heating, but this comes at the expense of reduced vehicle range. In cold weather, such heaters may continuously consume several kilowatts (kW) of electrical energy.

In comparative tests of heat pumps, several independent winter assessments have shown that in sub-zero conditions, electric vehicles equipped solely with PTC heaters experience a reduction in range of between 28 per cent and 54 per cent of their rated range. It is for this very reason that car buyers are turning their attention to heat pump technology; the objective reality is that, whilst PTC heaters are reliable, they are not an efficient choice.

## Air Heaters and Coolant Heaters: Different Functions

PTC heaters are broadly divided into two main categories, and this is precisely where buyers are most likely to become confused when making a purchase.

Air-type PTC heaters are installed within heating, ventilation and air-conditioning (HVAC) ducts. Cold air flows directly through the ceramic heating elements and is then delivered into the passenger compartment. This heating method offers a rapid response: warm air is delivered within seconds of switch-on, without the need to preheat a liquid circulation circuit.

Its sole function is to enhance comfort within the passenger compartment. Our [high-voltage PTC air heaters](https://evbtms.com/high-voltage-ptc-air-heater/) fall into this category.

Coolant-type PTC heaters (such as our [HVCH series](https://evbtms.com/products/high-voltage-coolant-heater/)) heat the ethylene glycol circulation circuit rather than the air. The heated coolant can be directed either to the HVAC heating core to warm the passenger compartment, or to the battery pack for preheating, or even to fulfil both requirements simultaneously. Although its response time is not as fast as that of direct air heating, it can meet multiple load requirements simultaneously with a single heater, making it particularly suitable for vehicles already equipped with liquid thermal management systems. Battery preheating is crucial for vehicles: in cold conditions, the battery pack charges more slowly and its power output is limited; by heating the coolant, the battery pack can be restored to an appropriate operating temperature range.

The choice of heater does not depend on which is better or worse, but rather on the vehicle’s existing system configuration. If a vehicle is fitted with a liquid circulation circuit and requires battery preheating, a coolant-type heater should be selected; if the vehicle merely needs to rapidly raise the air temperature in the passenger compartment, an air-type heater should be chosen.

Before delving deeper into the subject, it is important to clarify the type of circulation circuit the heater will serve, as this directly determines the required power range and control method.

## PTC and Heat Pumps: Their Respective Applications

Behind most enquiries regarding PTC, the core question is actually: should one choose a PTC heater or a heat pump system? The answer depends on the vehicle’s operational requirements in extremely cold conditions. In mildly cold climates, heat pump systems offer higher energy efficiency. They are capable of transferring heat from the outside air, the motor, the inverter and even the battery pack into the passenger compartment. Under mild conditions, for every unit of electrical energy consumed, a heat pump can generate several times that amount in heat. However, as the outside temperature drops (typically to -10°C to -15°C or below), the heat pump’s heating capacity diminishes, and it may ultimately be unable to meet heating requirements on its own.

In contrast, PTC heaters do not suffer from this performance degradation. Whether the ambient temperature is –30°C or 0°C, their output remains constant, as they generate heat through their own operation (rather than via heat transfer). For this reason, in cold climates, PTC heaters have become the standard auxiliary or primary heat source for electric vehicles—they continue to function reliably even under extreme weather conditions.

Many vehicles are equipped with both systems: the heat pump operates in milder climates, helping to extend the driving range, whilst the PTC heater is responsible for coping with severe cold and providing rapid heating. For users who only drive in regions with mild winter climates, a heat pump system is sufficient; however, for vehicles that need to start in temperatures as low as –25°C (such as buses), a PTC heater is an essential feature.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is PTC heating?**
PTC heating is a form of resistive heating whose core component is made of ceramic material; its resistance increases as the temperature rises. The current generates heat, the heat causes the resistance to rise, and this increase in resistance in turn reduces the current. Consequently, the heater is able to self-regulate its output power without relying entirely on an external thermostat.

**Do PTC heaters consume a lot of electricity?**
Yes, compared to heat pump systems, they consume a relatively large amount of electricity. Under ideal conditions, a PTC heater can convert at most 1 unit of electrical energy into 1 unit of thermal energy. In cold weather, cabin heating may draw several kilowatts (kW) of power from the power battery; this is precisely why vehicles equipped solely with PTC heating experience a significant reduction in range during winter. However, this energy consumption is offset by reliability in extremely cold conditions and the ability to heat the cabin instantly.

**Can PTC heaters overheat?**
PTC elements have self-limiting temperature characteristics: as the temperature rises, their resistance increases and the current decreases accordingly; therefore, they do not experience uncontrolled temperature rises in the same way as fixed-resistance heating coils. However, this does not replace the vehicle’s own protective mechanisms. The operation of the heater is still governed by the vehicle’s control and safety systems, and installation must comply with the rated voltage and current requirements.

**Are all electric vehicles fitted with PTC heaters?**
Not necessarily. Many newer electric vehicles utilise heat pumps as their primary heating method, whilst retaining PTC heaters as a backup to cope with extremely cold weather or to achieve rapid heating. Some older models or budget electric vehicles, however, are often fitted with PTC heaters alone. Buses and lorries operating in cold regions are almost universally fitted with PTC heaters, as heat pumps alone cannot meet heating requirements in extremely cold conditions.

**Are PTC heaters the same as high-voltage coolant heaters?**
Not exactly. High-voltage coolant heaters are a type of PTC heater specifically designed to heat the coolant circulation circuit, whilst air-type PTC heaters directly heat the air inside the passenger compartment. Both are based on the same PTC principle. Coolant-type heaters can also be used for battery preheating, which air-type heaters cannot achieve.

**If an electric vehicle is already equipped with a heat pump, is a PTC heater still necessary?**
Generally, yes. In extremely cold conditions, the efficiency of a heat pump decreases; when temperatures fall below approximately −10°C, some manufacturers retain a PTC heater as a backup for rapid heating and defrosting. Laboratory tests have shown that under cold start conditions at −10°C, the energy consumption of a heat pump is actually higher than that of a PTC heater; however, once the cabin temperature rises, the heat pump’s energy efficiency surpasses that of the PTC heater. For fleets operating in cold regions, the two are usually used in combination.
