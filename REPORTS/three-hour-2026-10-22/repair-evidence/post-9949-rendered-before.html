How Integrated Thermal Management Controllers Reduce the Cost of Electric Bus Systems

According to data from Dataintelo, the electric bus thermal management market is projected to grow from approximately US$3.2 billion in 2025 to US$8.7 billion in 2034, at a compound annual growth rate of 11.8 per cent. Within this market, compressors account for nearly 29 per cent of the total component value, heat exchangers for 24 per cent, and heating, ventilation and air conditioning (HVAC) controllers for over 21 per cent. As the scale of applications expands, cost pressures have shifted from individual components to the system architecture level—specifically issues relating to the number of controllers, the extent of cabling and the workload involved in validation.

One of the most obvious approaches to architectural optimisation is ‘integration’—merging the electric compressor controller, the refrigeration system’s electronic control unit (ECU) and the high-pressure PTC heater controller into a single integrated thermal management controller. Whilst the industry generally recognises that integration can ‘save costs’, few have provided specific quantitative analyses. This article will explore this issue in depth: where exactly are the costs incurred in the traditional ‘three-controller’ architecture? And which specific components can be eliminated through a ‘three-in-one’ design?

Traditional Electric Bus Thermal Management Architecture and the Reasons for Its High Costs

A typical electric bus thermal management system requires three control functions; in traditional solutions, each function usually corresponds to a separate control unit:

  • Compressor controller: Responsible for driving the electric compressor, incorporating a variable-frequency DC-AC drive circuit, power stage, gate driver and current sensing functions.
  • Refrigeration system ECU: Responsible for managing the refrigerant circuit, including reading data from pressure and temperature sensors, and driving the electronic expansion valve, water valves and water pump.
  • High-voltage PTC controller: Responsible for switching and regulating the heating elements in the passenger compartment and battery pack, equipped with a dedicated high-voltage switching stage and safety monitoring functions.

Three control units, three different part numbers, and they may even come from three different suppliers. Viewed in isolation, the engineering design of each individual controller may well be perfectly sound. The crux of the cost issue lies not with any specific controller, but with the complexity resulting from this ‘triple layering’.

Each controller requires its own housing, PCB, connectors, low-voltage power supply input, CAN node within the vehicle network, as well as its own calibration and validation processes. Taken individually, these elements may seem insignificant; however, when they are ‘multiplied by three’ across the entire system, the cost implications become substantial.

Where exactly does the money go? – The five major cost categories

To gain insight into the true cost structure, it is helpful to break it down into five major categories. The purchase price of the controller itself is merely one of them.

  1. Controller hardware (in triplicate). This includes the housing, printed circuit boards (PCBs), connectors and power stage circuits. This entails three sets of mechanical components, three electronic manufacturing processes and three sets of incoming inspection procedures. Even without any software development involved, this duplication of mechanical and electrical components in itself represents a tangible cost.
  2. Wiring harnesses. These encompass high-voltage feeders, low-voltage power supply lines and CAN bus branches—involving copper usage, weight and connectors. Every interface between devices requires a pair of connectors, and each pair represents both a cost and a potential point of failure. The cost of wiring harnesses depends not only on the length of the wires but is also directly proportional to the number of devices (controllers).
  3. Labour costs for installation. This involves three installation operations, three wiring tasks, and three rounds of commissioning and end-of-line testing on the vehicle. On a production line manufacturing hundreds of coaches annually, the difference in labour costs between installing one controller and installing three can be calculated through simple arithmetic.
  4. Validation costs. These include design validation, production validation and electromagnetic compatibility (EMC) testing—all of which must be carried out separately for each controller. Three independent units mean three rounds of EMC testing, three sets of environmental test protocols, and three sets of documentation for type approval. Validation costs are often underestimated, as they are typically allocated to the engineering budget rather than the bill of materials (BOM); however, in reality, every platform project must bear this expense.
  5. Quality assurance costs. The more interfaces there are, the more potential points of failure: connectors may corrode, wiring harnesses may wear out, and CAN nodes may lose connection. On-site faults, time spent on diagnostics and the need to replace components all increase with the complexity of the system, rather than depending solely on the quality of individual components.
  • Third-party evidence supports the trend towards ‘integration’ (although the specific figures may not correspond exactly in the case of thermal management controllers). In its research on integrated power electronics, IDTechEx found that by sharing enclosures, passive components, copper materials, control circuits and cooling systems, integrated solutions can offer cost savings of up to 25 per cent compared to discrete designs—we cite this figure here merely as an analogy from the power electronics sector, rather than as a measured value for thermal management controllers.Case studies on integration from suppliers such as INFAC and Vicor also highlight the same advantages: a reduction in high-voltage cables and connectors, reduced cooling redundancy, fewer brackets and enclosures, a shorter bill of materials (BOM), fewer potential leakage points, simplified wiring harness layout, and improved manufacturing consistency.In a forward-looking report on thermal management for new energy vehicles (NEVs), ResearchAndMarkets explicitly states, with regard to our area of focus, that integrating the separate drivers for expansion valves, water pumps and water valves into a thermal management controller not only reduces system costs but also significantly lowers the failure rate of the electronic control units (ECUs) for each component.

    The logic behind integration – what does the ‘three-in-one’ solution eliminate?

    Next, let us examine this from another perspective. The so-called ‘three-in-one’ thermal management controller—which integrates the compressor controller, refrigeration system ECU and high-voltage PTC controller into a single unit—eliminates previously redundant components:

    • A unified housing requires only a single sealing and validation process. By employing a single sealing design and a single set of environmental validation procedures, an IP67 protection rating can be achieved without the need for three separate validation processes.
    • Unified low-voltage power supply. A single 24V power rail (16–32V DC input) supplies power to the entire unit, replacing the original three independent low-voltage inputs, three sets of low-voltage connectors and three sets of low-voltage wiring harness branches.
    • Unified CAN node. The unit appears as a single node (CAN 2.0) within the vehicle network, rather than three independent nodes, thereby simplifying network configuration and diagnostic procedures.
    • Shared cooling system. The housing design features open cooling fins, enabling the entire unit’s thermal requirements to be met through natural air cooling—eliminating the need for three separate cold plates or air ducts. (The installation location must ensure an airflow of at least 3.5 m/s; this is a vehicle layout requirement that must be confirmed during the system integration phase.)
    • Fewer connectors reduce the risk of failure. Every pair of connectors eliminated not only saves costs but also removes a potential point of leakage or electrical failure—a finding that aligns with ResearchAndMarkets’ conclusions on ‘reducing ECU failure rates’.
    • A single high-voltage input is employed. Depending on the vehicle platform, the high-voltage input can be selected as 250–750 V DC or 600–1000 V DC, thereby accommodating 600 V and 800 V vehicle platforms; there is no need to configure three separate high-voltage connections.

    Furthermore, this architecture offers advantages at the control level—whilst difficult to quantify, the principle is self-evident: the three control loops—compressor speed, expansion valve opening and fan speed—are integrated within a single unit, sharing sensor data and eliminating communication delays between controllers. We shall elaborate in detail on how these control loops work in concert in a separate article exploring thermal management control strategies for electric vehicles.

    Reliability is not merely a quality metric, but a key lever influencing costs

    When discussing costs, people often focus solely on the bill of materials (BOM), but this is not a comprehensive view. Enhancing reliability through integration has a direct impact on warranty provisions, after-sales service costs and fleet uptime (i.e. vehicle availability):

    • Fewer control interfaces mean a lower system failure rate. This is the manufacturer’s assessment of the design approach, the logic of which stems directly from the aforementioned calculations regarding the number of connectors and interfaces.
    • Electromagnetic compatibility (EMC) can be uniformly optimised at the integrated unit level, eliminating the need to ensure that three independent components not only meet their respective standards but also coexist harmoniously, as would be required when dealing with three separate units. Only one EMC test is required, rather than three; this also reduces the potential for mutual interference at the vehicle level.
    • A unified diagnostic mechanism. A single controller implies a unified diagnostic interface and fault code system, which helps to pinpoint the root cause of faults more quickly in practical applications.

The range of spare parts required for maintenance has been reduced from three types to one. For fleet operators who stock parts across multiple warehouses, this means lower stockholding costs and simplified logistics processes—which are benefits that go beyond mere quality considerations.

Integration comes at no cost

The most common concern regarding integration is a loss of flexibility: ‘If I adopt an integrated solution, will I be forced to accept only the options you provide?’ For this controller architecture, the answer is no—the specific details are as follows:

  • Support for CAN bus customisation. The CAN protocol, CAN ID, baud rate and control logic can all be customised according to project requirements, enabling the unit to adapt to the vehicle’s existing network architecture without the need to redesign the vehicle system.
  • Compatibility with multiple compressors. The controller can be adapted to the compressor of the customer’s choice; the customer need only provide the technical specifications of the compressor and motor, and the driver can be configured accordingly. You are not restricted to a single compressor model.
  • The cooling solution is mature and reliable, rather than an experimental design. Utilising silicon carbide (SiC) power devices, combined with an open-fin housing design, natural air cooling alone is sufficient to handle the thermal load—no liquid cooling plates are required, nor is there any need to maintain additional pump circulation circuits.
  • It offers excellent environmental resilience. The unit achieves an IP67 protection rating and operates within an ambient temperature range of –40 °C to +65 °C, fully meeting the climatic requirements of electric bus operations. Weighing 6 kg, the unit supports vertical installation.

In short: standardisation applies to those repetitive infrastructure elements—such as enclosures, power supply units, network nodes and cooling systems—whilst all project-specific elements remain highly flexible.

How to Evaluate Integrated Thermal Management Controllers

If you are evaluating integrated thermal management controllers for bus or commercial vehicle platforms, we recommend confirming the following points with your supplier:

  • Voltage platform coverage. Does the unit support your 600 V or 800 V platform? Please confirm its high-voltage (HV) input range (e.g. 250–750 V DC or 600–1000 V DC) based on your power battery pack specifications.
  • Compressor adaptation process. What parameters does the supplier require from you? How is the driver configured for your specific compressor?
  • Transparency of control logic. Are you able to understand how the compressor, expansion valve and fan circuits operate? Can this logic be calibrated to suit your system?
  • Scope of CAN bus customisation. Communication protocols, identifiers (IDs) and baud rates—do these need to align with your vehicle network, or are they fixed configurations?
  • Environmental protection rating – IP67 protection rating and operating temperature range must be verified through actual testing, rather than relying solely on the manufacturer’s claims.
  • Project calibration support – Fan curves, pressure thresholds and control parameters tailored specifically to your system, rather than applying generic default settings.

Simply consulting a product catalogue is often insufficient. Selecting the appropriate controller depends on your vehicle platform, voltage architecture, compressor selection and the climatic conditions of the operating environment.
