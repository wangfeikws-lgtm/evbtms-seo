# Selection Guide for High-Voltage PTC Heaters for Electric Vehicles

The choice of a high-voltage PTC heater should be based on the heating function it is required to perform, not on its rated power in kilowatts. Determine what the battery system requires — preheating, cabin heating or both — under real-world operating conditions, and then match the heater’s voltage platform, power range, control interface and coolant circuit to that function. Ensure that these four aspects are agreed in writing before approving samples, and most integration issues will never arise.

A high-voltage coolant heater (HVCH) is the PTC heater that heats the coolant for battery pre-heating and passenger compartment heating in electric buses, lorries, mining trucks and construction machinery. Our range covers power bands of 6–18 kW, 20–25 kW and 30–35 kW on 400 V, 600 V and 800 V platforms, with customised ranges available on request. This guide outlines the same checks we ask buyers to carry out before quoting for a configuration.

## Define the preheating task before determining the rated power

First, check with the battery supplier regarding the applicable temperature range and operating limits. Clarify the intended use: battery preheating, cabin heating, or a combination of both. Note the battery’s initial state, ambient conditions, time available for preheating, and the vehicle’s operating mode. The power available whilst the vehicle is parked and charging may differ from that available whilst it is in motion; therefore, the definition of the task must cover both scenarios.

Never calculate heating time based solely on the rated power stated on the type plate. The battery’s mass, initial temperature, heat loss, coolant distribution and available electrical power all influence the final result. Heating objectives and test boundary conditions must be agreed jointly with the battery team and the vehicle integration team before ordering any samples.

## Adjust the voltage and power to the same operating point

Determine the vehicle’s minimum and maximum supply voltages for each relevant operating mode. Compare these values with the operating limits of the selected heater configuration and do not rely solely on the platform’s rated values, such as 400 V or 800 V. Ask suppliers to specify the voltage, coolant inlet temperature and flow rate conditions under which the stated heat output was measured.

Our heaters are available with a power output of 15 to 35 kW per unit, with platform voltages of up to 870 V DC, and have been in series production since 2021. For reference, 400 V and 800 V are standard reference values for electric vehicle platforms, and Webasto states that its HVH 50/70 units have a power output of between 5 and 7 kW. The power range and platform voltage are a single decision, not two: a heater that fits the voltage range but cannot provide the necessary heat in the coldest start-up conditions is an unsuitable heater, however impressive its technical specifications may seem.

System integrators must also verify the electrical power budget. Where other vehicle loads share the same power supply, the rated output stated on the type plate does not correspond to the power actually available for heating at any given moment. The selection of cables, connectors and protective devices must comply with the approved electrical design of the vehicle.

## Check the control interface: CAN or thermostat

High-voltage heating power and low-voltage control power must be kept separate. Obtain the diagrams for connectors, power requirements and interface details for the chosen model. Some configurations use CAN communication, whilst others use a thermostat. Do not assume that all heaters use the same interface.

Our B series uses CAN control with an enable signal, while our E series uses a thermostat and has no CAN. Clarify who issues the heating request, the signals that confirm the actual operating status, which controller provides information on the operating limits, and how the vehicle should respond if communication is lost. Sharing a CAN bus does not constitute a complete control strategy. In the proposed project, please confirm the definitions of the signals, the software versions and the diagnostic procedures in writing.

## Check the installation of the cooling circuit

A heater is a resistive element in a cooling circuit, which may also include a water pump, hoses, valves, cold plates and other heat exchangers. Ask the supplier for the pressure drop data for this heater for the specific coolant type, temperature and flow conditions. Compare the requirements of the entire circuit with the pump's performance curve.

Where the circuit contains parallel branches, the total flow rate of the pump differs from the flow rate in the heater branch. Clarify how the flow rate through the selected branch is measured in each operating mode. For cold start conditions, consult the pump and heater suppliers to confirm which data apply to the selected coolant and initial temperature conditions.

## Validate the samples in the installed system

Before commencing testing, the teams involved must agree on the initial condition of the battery and the coolant, the location of the sensors, the operating modes and the acceptance criteria. A standard time reference must be adopted when recording the demands and response of the heaters, the power supply conditions, the coolant inlet and outlet temperatures, and the heater branch flow rates.

Assess steady-state heating and the transition between operating modes. Where measured data deviates from expectations, retain the raw data and verify the interfaces and test limits in consultation with the relevant suppliers. Never bypass protective functions to obtain ideal results; fault injection must only be carried out within the framework of authorised test procedures.

Our Q-series heaters feature documented laboratory validation activities covering vibration assessment, water circulation and operational performance at Shanghai University of Engineering Science. Validation of samples within your installed system remains important: laboratory results describe the heater, whilst your vehicle describes the installation.

## Confirm the production configuration

Once the sample has been accepted, the confirmed reference numbers, drawing versions, connector layout, applicable control interfaces and any customer-specific options must be documented. System manufacturers and heater suppliers must agree on a mechanism for communicating changes and clarify which changes require further technical review.

Demand forecasts and anticipated lead times may serve as a reference for the procurement department. For specific orders, actual production capacity, lead times and commercial terms must be confirmed. The approval of a sample does not imply that other versions of hardware or software are equivalent.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is a high-voltage PTC heater?** A high-voltage PTC heater is an electric heater that heats the coolant or air in an electric vehicle, using a positive temperature coefficient element whose resistance increases as it heats up. In electric vehicle applications, it is typically used for battery pre-heating and cabin heating, and operates on the vehicle's high-voltage platform rather than on the 12 V or 24 V system.

**How does a coolant heater differ from an air heater?** A coolant heater heats the fluid in a cooling circuit that transfers heat to the battery or the cabin heat exchanger. An air heater heats the air directly. Coolant heaters are suitable for battery thermal management, as the same circuit serves the battery, whilst air heaters are suitable only for the passenger compartment. The selection criteria differ: coolant heaters require verification of pressure drop and flow rate, whilst air heaters require verification of air flow.

**What voltage rating do I need?** Match the heater to your vehicle’s actual supply voltages in all operating modes, not to the nominal values stated on the label. Our range includes 400 V, 600 V and 800 V platforms with customised power ranges from 15 to 35 kW per unit and platform voltages of up to 870 V DC. Ask the supplier to specify the conditions under which the rated power was measured.

**CAN control or thermostat control?** It depends on your vehicle’s architecture. CAN control with an activation signal is suitable for vehicles that manage heating via a central controller. Thermostat control is suitable for simpler installations that do not have a CAN node for heating. Before making your choice, confirm who issues the heating request and how the vehicle responds in the event of a communication fault.

**What should I ask the supplier before approving the samples?** Request details of the heating task, the voltage and power at the same operating point, pressure drop data for your coolant and the temperatures, documentation on connectors and interfaces, and the process for communicating changes. Agree on the test conditions and acceptance criteria before testing begins, not after the results prove disappointing.

**Do you supply a complete heating system or just the heater?** We supply the heater and assist with integration into your vehicle team: configuration selection, interface documentation and sample validation. System-level components, such as pumps, hoses and cold plates, are specified jointly for your project. Please send us the details of your application to receive a written quotation.
