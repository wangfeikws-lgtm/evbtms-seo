<p>"But the stack makes waste heat. Why are we buying a cabin heater at all?" That turns up at the first meeting of nearly every fuel cell bus project we talk to. Fair question. It survives until the first cold start, and not much longer.</p>

<p>Our answer never changes: specify a PTC coolant heater in the 20 kW class from the start, and treat stack waste heat as an offset, never as the heating system. Fleets running hydrogen buses through northern winters settled this years ago. The Beijing Winter Olympics fleets were the public example: waste-heat recovery plus an electric heater on every bus.</p>

<h2>The stack starts the morning as a heating load</h2>

<p>Begin with temperature. A diesel engine is a furnace that also turns wheels; its coolant comes off hot. A PEM fuel cell is efficient — the entire point of it — and its coolant runs cooler. Then the cold start: the stack is a cold mass, needing warming itself before it can work hard. For those first minutes it is not a heat source. It is one more load in the queue.</p>

<p>The bus has sat outside all night. The driver does his checks in the dark, windscreen opaque, first passengers waiting. The stack wants heat. The glass wants heat. The cabin has not warmed a degree. That is the moment the heating system was bought for. Not noon. Not cruise. Then.</p>

<h2>Cruise is the easy part of the route</h2>

<p>Published studies of fuel cell buses on winter cycles around −7°C show a consistent pattern: at steady cruise, stack waste heat covers part of the cabin load; drop into the low-load segments and an electric auxiliary heater carries the rest — most of it.</p>

<p>City duty is mostly low-load segments. Idling at a terminus. Crawling between stops. Doors opening at every stop while the stack sips hydrogen, makes little heat, and twenty passengers expect a warm cabin. Size it for cruise and it fails at the terminus daily, in front of the people it was bought for. Size it honestly and you have specified a PTC heater.</p>

<h2>The water reaches projects late</h2>

<p>One detail arrives late in these projects: the water. Fuel cell coolant is not ordinary coolant. The stack loop must stay at low electrical conductivity, so it runs deionised water with an ion exchanger, and anything that leaches ions into it starts a warranty argument. So a PTC heater rarely sits inside the stack loop. Usually the heater gets its own loop, or couples through a heat exchanger.</p>

<p>Which raises decisions teams meet too late: which loop the heater belongs to, who controls it, what happens to cabin heat if the stack faults. Decide it on paper. Deciding it during winter validation costs a season.</p>

<h2>The load sum leaves a 20 kW hole</h2>

<p>Do the arithmetic for a 12-metre city bus in northern service, item by item. First, the steady drain: heat leaking through panels and glass, a load that never stops. Second, the doors: every stop dumps warmed air on the pavement and pulls cold air in, every few hundred metres. Third, defrost: brief, front-loaded, not negotiable — the bus does not move until the driver can see. Add the three and the total lands in the 15–25 kW band.</p>

<p>Waste heat, when available, offsets part of that. What remains, on the worst morning not the average one, is a heater in the 20 kW class. That is why the figure recurs. It is not fashion. It is the size of the hole the stack leaves. Our E Series sits there, 20–24 kW at 400–800 V; larger cabins and colder routes step up a series, into Q.</p>

<h2>What we would specify</h2>

<p>For a 12-metre fuel cell city bus in cold-climate service: a PTC coolant heater in the 20 kW class on its own controlled loop, waste-heat recovery plumbed to offset it, CAN control so the energy manager can ration heat against hydrogen and route, and defrost-first priority after departure. Then validate on the coldest route, at the coldest hour, fully loaded. Averages heat nobody.</p>

<h2>Questions we hear on this topic</h2>

<h3>So on a good day, could the stack heat the bus by itself?</h3>
<p>On a mild day it can get close, and the heater may barely run. What it cannot cover is the gaps: cold starts, when the stack is warming itself, and low-load stretches, when there is little heat to recover. Those gaps are exactly when the cabin load bites. So the heater stays on the specification. Fitted, plumbed, mostly idle on the good days.</p>

<h3>Why not just bolt on a diesel parking heater and be done with it?</h3>
<p>Early fleets tried that, and thermally it works fine. The problem is the story: a bus sold to a city as zero-emission, burning diesel at the kerb, is a hard sell, and several zones restrict it outright. Electric heat keeps the emissions claim true. For most of these projects, that claim is the reason the bus exists.</p>

<h3>Fine. But doesn't a 20 kW heater eat the range?</h3>
<p>It draws real energy, out of the hydrogen or the buffer battery. We will not pretend it is free. But set it against the alternative: a bus that cannot clear its windscreen does not enter service at all. Precondition at the depot. Blend waste heat whenever the stack offers it. Let the energy manager ration the rest. The cost stays honest, and the bus works.</p>

<p><strong>Specifying a fuel cell bus for cold service?</strong> Send us the bus length, the coldest operating condition, the defrost requirement and the stack coolant architecture. We will size the heater and flag the loop questions before winter testing.</p>
