<p>The air heater quote is always cheaper. We manufacture coolant heaters, so you should discount what follows accordingly, and then check the arithmetic anyway, because the purchase price is the smallest line in this comparison. Over the life of a commercial EV, the two architectures bill you in different places: installation labour, a second heating system you may not have budgeted, energy, and downtime in the month you can least afford it.</p>

<h2>Line one: purchase price, where air wins</h2>

<p>An air PTC heater is a heating element, a fan and a housing. A coolant heater is a pressure-rated liquid device with high-voltage connectors, seals and a control board, and it expects a pump and plumbing around it. Unit for unit, air is cheaper to buy and cheaper to ship. Any supplier who argues otherwise is selling. Round one to air, honestly.</p>

<h2>Line two: installation, where it depends on the vehicle</h2>

<p>Air heat needs ducting and high-voltage wiring to where the people are. Coolant heat needs hoses, fittings, a bleed procedure and a place in the liquid loop. Here is the part quotes leave out: on a modern commercial EV, a liquid loop already exists. The battery is liquid-cooled, the loop is routed, the pump is fitted, the coolant is on the service schedule. Adding a heater to that loop is incremental work. On an older or air-cooled platform with no loop at all, the coolant heater drags an entire circuit in behind it, and the air heater wins the installation line outright.</p>

<h2>Line three: the second job, where the comparison flips</h2>

<p>An air heater heats air. It cannot warm a liquid-cooled battery, full stop. In a cold climate, the battery needs heat anyway: for charge acceptance in the morning, for power, for longevity. So the air-heater vehicle in a northern fleet buys its battery heating separately, as a second system, with a second control integration and a second failure mode.</p>

<p>The coolant heater does both jobs through one loop and a set of valves. It warms the pack before departure, then serves the cabin on the road. When people ask us why coolant heaters dominate commercial EVs despite the higher unit price, this is the answer. It is not the heater that is cheaper. It is the second heater you never buy.</p>

<h2>Line four: energy, where nobody wins</h2>

<p>Both technologies turn electricity into heat at roughly one to one. Twenty kilowatts for one hour is twenty kilowatt-hours either way, and any claim of a dramatic efficiency gap between resistive air and resistive coolant heating is marketing. The real differences are second-order: a coolant system can precondition the pack and cabin on depot power before departure, spending grid energy instead of battery energy, and it can later blend in motor waste heat through the same loop. An air heater warms the cabin quickly and loses the heat just as quickly when the doors open and the fan stops. On a city bus duty cycle, that response difference shows up in the energy log. It is a few percent, not a revolution.</p>

<h2>Line five: maintenance and the January failure</h2>

<p>Coolant heaters ride on a circuit the fleet already maintains; coolant changes, hose inspections and pump life are known quantities. Air heater elements live in dusty ducts and see thermal shock every cold start. Neither is maintenance-free, whatever the brochures say.</p>

<p>The downtime line matters more than either. A heater failure in January takes the vehicle out of service under both architectures. What differs is the fleet's position afterwards: one heating system type across the fleet means one spare on the shelf and one diagnostic routine the technicians know. Fleets that split architectures for small savings usually pay it back in spares and training.</p>

<p>One more line never appears in the spreadsheet: drivers. A cab that warms slowly on a January morning generates complaints, workarounds and eventually a request to idle something that should not idle. Heating that arrives before departure, because the vehicle preconditioned on the plug, is the version nobody writes emails about.</p>

<h2>Our verdict, with the bias declared</h2>

<p>Choose air when the vehicle has no liquid loop, the climate is mild, the job is cabin-only, or the project is a retrofit where plumbing is genuinely impractical. Small vans and spot-heating for a sleeper cab are fair examples. Choose coolant when the battery is liquid-cooled, winters are real, or the vehicle must precondition on shore power, which is most commercial EV programmes we see. Run the five lines above on your own duty cycle before you run them on price.</p>

<h2>Questions we hear on this topic</h2>

<h3>Is a coolant heater worth it on a small electric van?</h3>
<p>If the van's battery is liquid-cooled and it works in cold weather, usually yes, because the battery heating job is already there waiting. If the van is air-cooled and southern, an air heater is the rational buy.</p>

<h3>Does a coolant heater warm the cabin more slowly?</h3>
<p>Slightly, on a fully cold start, because the loop itself has to warm. In practice the difference is minutes, and preconditioning while plugged in removes it entirely.</p>

<h3>Should we compare either option against a heat pump?</h3>
<p>Yes, on any new platform. Heat pumps cut heating energy in moderate cold and lose ground in deep cold, which is why many vehicles pair one with a PTC heater. We compared the two in detail in our heat pump article.</p>
