## HTML body

```html
<p>If you are comparing an air heater with a coolant heater, compare the installed system rather than just two component quotations. Cabin heat, battery conditioning, controls, maintenance and energy use may sit on different lines of the project budget. No architecture is always the lower-cost choice.</p>

<h2>Line one: compare equivalent purchase scope</h2>

<p>An air PTC heater transfers heat into an airflow; a coolant heater transfers heat into a liquid circuit. Compare units with equivalent output, voltage, controls and environmental requirements. Include fans, pumps, ducts, hoses, valves and installation hardware as applicable. Supplier quotations are needed to establish which complete system costs less.</p>

<h2>Line two: installation, where it depends on the vehicle</h2>

<p>Air heating needs suitable airflow and ducting. Coolant heating needs a compatible circuit, heat exchanger, pump and service access. An existing battery coolant loop may reduce integration work, but it is not automatically suitable for cabin heat: temperature levels, fluid compatibility, pressure drop and isolation requirements still need checking.</p>

<h2>Line three: account for battery heating</h2>

<p>A cabin air heater does not directly heat coolant in a battery circuit. If the battery requires cold-weather heating, include the selected battery-conditioning method in the cost comparison. That method may already exist on the vehicle; do not automatically count it as a new system.</p>

<p>A shared coolant-heating arrangement may support battery and cabin duties if its temperatures, capacity and control strategy suit both. Compare shared and separated circuits explicitly. Combining functions can change component count, but it does not guarantee that a second heater or circuit can be eliminated.</p>

<h2>Line four: measure energy over the same duty cycle</h2>

<p>For an illustrative constant electrical input of 20 kW over one hour, input energy is 20 kWh. Useful heat delivered to passengers or a pack can still differ because of thermal mass, distribution losses, auxiliary loads and controls. Compare energy at equivalent comfort and battery-temperature targets, including preconditioning. No percentage saving is claimed here.</p>

<h2>Line five: maintenance and the January failure</h2>

<p>Coolant heaters ride on a circuit the fleet already maintains; coolant changes, hose inspections and pump life are known quantities. Air heater elements live in dusty ducts and see thermal shock every cold start. Neither is maintenance-free, whatever the brochures say.</p>

<p>Include service access, spare-parts coverage, diagnostic tools and repair time in the downtime allowance. Shared components can simplify stockholding but can also create common failure paths. Fleet records and maintenance plans—not the heater category alone—should determine the allowance.</p>

<p>One more line never appears in the spreadsheet: drivers. A cab that warms slowly on a January morning generates complaints, workarounds and eventually a request to idle something that should not idle. Heating that arrives before departure, because the vehicle preconditioned on the plug, is the version nobody writes emails about.</p>

<h2>Our verdict, with the bias declared</h2>

<p>Choose air when the vehicle has no liquid loop, the climate is mild, the job is cabin-only, or the project is a retrofit where plumbing is genuinely impractical. Small vans and spot-heating for a sleeper cab are fair examples. Choose coolant when the battery is liquid-cooled, winters are real, or the vehicle must precondition on shore power, which is most commercial EV programmes we see. Run the five lines above on your own duty cycle before you run them on price.</p>

<h2>Questions we hear on this topic</h2>

<h3>Is a coolant heater worth it on a small electric van?</h3>
<p>If the van's battery is liquid-cooled and it works in cold weather, usually yes, because the battery heating job is already there waiting. If the van is air-cooled and southern, an air heater is the rational buy.</p>

<h3>Does a coolant heater warm the cabin more slowly?</h3>
<p>A coolant system may need to warm its liquid and heat exchangers before useful cabin heat arrives. Actual response depends on circuit volume, heater output, airflow and controls. Compare measured warm-up curves at the same starting conditions; no fixed time difference is claimed.</p>

<h3>Should we compare either option against a heat pump?</h3>
<p>Yes, on any new platform. Heat pumps cut heating energy in moderate cold and lose ground in deep cold, which is why many vehicles pair one with a PTC heater. We compared the two in detail in our heat pump article.</p>
```
