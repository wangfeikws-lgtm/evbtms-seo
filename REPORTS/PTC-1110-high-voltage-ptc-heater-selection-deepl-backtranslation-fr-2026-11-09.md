# Selection Guide for High-Voltage PTC Heaters for Electric Vehicles

When selecting a high-voltage PTC heater, the primary consideration should be the heating function to be performed, rather than the rated power in kilowatts. Determine the requirements of the battery system — pre-heating, cabin heating or both — under real-world operating conditions, then adapt the heater’s voltage range, power range, control interface and cooling circuit to suit this function. Record these four parameters in writing before approving the samples, and most integration issues will never arise.

A high-voltage coolant heater (HVCH) is a PTC heater that heats the coolant for battery pre-heating and cabin heating in electric buses, lorries, mining trucks and construction machinery. Our range covers power ratings from 6 to 18 kW, 20 to 25 kW and 30 to 35 kW on 400 V, 600 V and 800 V platforms, with customised ranges available on request. This guide sets out the same checks we ask buyers to carry out before we provide them with a quotation for a configuration.

## Define the heating task before determining the rated power

Start by asking the battery supplier for the applicable temperature range and operating limits. Specify the intended use: preheating the battery, heating the passenger compartment, or a combination of both. Note the battery’s initial state, environmental conditions, the available heating time and the vehicle’s operating mode. The power available when the vehicle is stationary and charging may differ from that available whilst it is in motion; the definition of the task must therefore cover both scenarios.

Never estimate the heating time based solely on the rated power indicated on the nameplate. The battery’s mass, its initial temperature, heat losses, coolant distribution and available electrical power all affect the final result. Heating objectives and test boundary conditions must be agreed jointly with the battery team and the vehicle integration team before any samples are ordered.

## Matching voltage and power at the same operating point

Determine the vehicle’s minimum and maximum supply voltages for each relevant operating mode. Compare these values with the operating limits of the selected heating configuration, and do not rely solely on the rated values shown on the platform labels, such as 400 V or 800 V. Ask suppliers to specify the voltage, coolant inlet temperature and flow rate conditions under which the heating power they quote was measured.

Our heaters are available in a range of 15 to 35 kW per unit, with platform voltages of up to 870 V DC, and have been in series production since 2021. For reference, 400 V and 800 V are the standard reference values for electric vehicle platforms, and Webasto specifies an output of 5 to 7 kW for its HVH 50/70 units. The power range and platform voltage constitute a single decision-making criterion, not two: a heater that falls within the voltage range but cannot provide the required heat under the coldest start-up conditions is not the right choice, however impressive its technical specifications may seem.

System integrators must also check the electrical power budget. When other vehicle loads share the same power supply, the rated power stated on the type plate does not correspond to the power actually available for the heating system at any given time. The selection of cables, connectors and protective devices must comply with the vehicle’s approved electrical design.

## Check the control interface: CAN or thermostat

The high-voltage power supply for the heater and the low-voltage power supply for the control unit must be separate. Obtain the connector diagrams, power supply requirements and interface information for the selected model. Some configurations use CAN communication; others use a thermostat. Do not assume that all heaters use the same interface.

Our B series uses a CAN control system with an activation signal; our E series uses a thermostat and does not have a CAN bus. Specify who issues the heating request, which signals confirm the actual operating status, which controller provides information on operating limits, and how the vehicle should respond in the event of a communication failure. Sharing a CAN bus does not in itself constitute a complete control strategy. Please confirm in writing the signal definitions, software versions and diagnostic procedures for the proposed project.

## Checking the cooling circuit installation

A heater is a resistive element integrated into a cooling circuit, which may also include a water pump, hoses, valves, cold plates and other heat exchangers. Ask the supplier for data on the pressure drop of this heater for the specific type of coolant, temperature and flow conditions. Compare the requirements of the entire circuit with the pump’s performance curve.

Where the circuit includes parallel branches, the total flow rate of the pump differs from the flow rate in the heater branch. Specify how the flow rate in the selected branch is measured for each operating mode. For cold start-up conditions, consult the pump and heater suppliers to confirm which data applies to the selected coolant and initial temperature conditions.

## A single, common interface document

Compile the agreed elements into a single document to be signed by both the system supplier and the heater supplier. Indicate any missing parameters as ‘to be determined’; suppliers cannot validate a complete system configuration based on an incomplete list of assumptions.

| Item to be reviewed | Information on which the system supplier must agree with the heater supplier |
|---|---|
| Heating requirement | Heat consumer, initial state, target and available timeframe |
| Power supply | Minimum and maximum voltage and available power in each mode |
| Cooling circuit | Fluid, concentration, flow rate in the heater branch, pressure and inlet conditions |
| Control | Request owner, interface, feedback, restrictions and fault management |
| Installation | Approved layout, connectors, assembly, orientation and access for maintenance |
| Verification | Hardware and software versions, test conditions and acceptance criteria |
| Procurement | Example configuration, documentation, communication of changes and bill of quantities |

## Validating samples in the installed system

Before testing begins, the teams involved must agree on the initial condition of the battery and the coolant, the location of the sensors, the operating modes and the acceptance criteria. Use a consistent time reference when recording requests and feedback from the heaters, power supply conditions, coolant inlet and outlet temperatures, and flow rates in the heater branches.

Evaluate steady-state heating and the transition between operating modes. Where measured data deviates from predictions, retain the raw data and verify the interfaces and test limits in collaboration with the relevant suppliers. Never bypass protective functions to obtain ideal results; fault injection tests must be carried out within the framework of authorised test procedures.

Our Q-series heaters have undergone documented laboratory validation tests covering vibrations, water flow and operational performance assessment, carried out at Shanghai University of Engineering Science. However, it is still important to carry out a validation test on a sample unit within your installed system: the laboratory results relate to the heater itself, whilst your vehicle reflects the actual installation conditions.

## Validate the production configuration

Once the prototype has been accepted, document the confirmed part numbers, drawing versions, connector layout, applicable control interfaces and any customer-specific options. System manufacturers and heater suppliers must agree on a mechanism for communicating changes and specify which changes require further technical review.

Demand forecasts and target lead times may serve as a reference for procurement. For specific orders, actual production capacity, lead times and commercial terms must be confirmed. Approval of a sample does not imply that other hardware or software versions are equivalent.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is a high-voltage PTC heater?** A high-voltage PTC heater is an electric heater that heats the coolant or air in an electric vehicle, using a positive temperature coefficient element whose resistance increases as it heats up. In electric vehicle applications, it is generally used for battery preheating and cabin heating, operating on the vehicle’s high-voltage platform rather than on the 12 V or 24 V system.

**How does a coolant heater differ from an air heater?** A coolant heater heats the fluid in a cooling circuit that transfers heat to the battery or the cabin heat exchanger. An air heater heats the air directly. Coolant heaters are suitable for battery thermal management as the same circuit serves the battery, whilst air heaters are intended exclusively for heating the passenger compartment. The selection criteria differ: coolant heaters require verification of pressure drop and flow rate, whilst air heaters require verification of air flow rate.

**What voltage do I need?** Choose the heater based on your vehicle’s actual supply voltages in all operating modes, rather than on the nominal value stated on the label. Our range covers voltages of 400 V, 600 V and 800 V with customisable power ranges from 15 to 35 kW per unit and voltages of up to 870 V DC. Ask the supplier to specify the conditions under which the rated power was measured.

**CAN control or thermostat control: which should you choose?** This depends on your vehicle’s architecture. CAN control with an activation signal is suitable for vehicles where the heating is managed by a central controller. Thermostat control is suitable for simpler systems that do not have a CAN node for the heating. Before making your choice, check who initiates the heating request and how the vehicle responds in the event of a communication failure.

**What should I ask the supplier before approving the samples?** Ask for the definition of the heating task, the voltage and power at the same operating point, the pressure drop data for your coolant and the temperatures, documentation relating to connectors and interfaces, as well as the process for communicating changes. Agree on the test conditions and acceptance criteria before testing begins, rather than after the results have proved disappointing.

**Do you supply a complete heating system or just the heater?** We supply the heater and support your vehicle engineering team with its integration: selecting the configuration, providing documentation on interfaces and validating the samples. System-level components, such as pumps, hoses and cooling plates, are defined jointly for your project. Please send us the details of your application to receive a written quotation.
