The email usually shows up a few days after the quote goes out. The buyer has the Q Series 15–35 kW coolant heater sheet open in front of them, two power ratings circled, and one question: which one do we actually need?

We do not answer with a model number. We ask three questions back. How heavy is the pack? How cold does the truck sit overnight? And once the driver shows up, how many minutes before that truck has to move?

Short answer, before the working: the departure window decides it more often than the truck does. A 35 kW unit is not an upgraded 15 kW unit. It is the same job done against a shorter clock.

Do the pack sum first

Take a long-haul tractor, pack in the 300–350 kWh class. Forget the heater for a moment and weigh the battery. A pack that size comes in at roughly two tonnes.

Now the heat. Cell specific heat sits near 1 kJ per kilogram per degree. Two thousand kilograms, lifted 20°C, at 1 kJ a kilo-degree: that is 40,000 kJ, or 40 megajoules — about 11 kWh of heat that has to physically be inside those cells. The coolant, housings and pipework take their cut on the way in, so treat 11 kWh as the floor, not the total.

Last step, divide by power. Hand that 11 kWh to a 15 kW heater and it needs around 45 minutes running flat out, longer once the losses above go in. Hand it to a 35 kW heater and the same lift is done in under 20 minutes.

That one division settles most of these enquiries.

Picture the truck cold-soaked at −25°C, driver on site, wheels turning 30 minutes later. The 15 kW unit never had a chance. Now park the identical truck indoors, on depot power with two hours to precondition. The maths flips completely: the extra capacity is money spent warming a building. Same truck, same pack, opposite answer. This is why we refuse to pick from the rating plate alone.

The cab rarely sizes the heater. The glass sometimes does.

Once everything is warm, holding a truck cab at temperature in deep cold only takes a few kilowatts. The cab is a small box. What costs power is the first ten minutes: cold seats, cold panels, and a windscreen that has to be clear before the wheels move. Defrost is a short, hard burst, landing at exactly the moment the battery is also asking for everything the heater has.

Buses break this pattern, and bus buyers should not borrow truck numbers. A 12-metre city bus cabin, doors opening every few hundred metres, can call for two to three times the heater capacity of a truck cab. We went through the cold-end planning side of that in our notes on electric bus battery preheating at −30°C.

Then ask what the wiring thinks

Power ratings look clean on paper. Current is where they get physical. A 15 kW heater at 400 V pulls close to 38 A. That is already not a trivial circuit, and it is the small unit.

The 35 kW unit shows the split clearly. At 800 V it draws about 44 A, manageable. At 400 V the same unit wants about 88 A, which means heavier cable, bigger fuses, and a harness conversation nobody enjoys late in a programme. This is the whole reason the Q Series spans 400–1100 V. On an 870 V platform the big unit stops being a packaging fight, and we have been shipping into DC870V truck programmes since 2021.

Mining trucks take the logic one step further. Nobody fits one enormous heater. Our installations parallel six 35 kW units for a combined 210 kW per vehicle, because the heat load genuinely is that large and a fitter can swap one standard unit at the roadside. Try doing that with a single bespoke giant.

Where we land, case by case

Fifteen kilowatts, when the climate is moderate, the pack is modest, and the truck comes home every night to a depot where it can precondition properly on shore power. Regional delivery work is the classic case, and we will happily sell the smaller unit there. Oversizing it helps no one.

Thirty-five, when any of these turn up: cold soaks below −20°C, a departure window under half an hour, a pack over 300 kWh, or a vehicle that has to heat while it works instead of while it charges. Mining and construction trucks tick every box on that list. Northern line-haul fleets sleeping outdoors tick most of them.

Guess wrong one way and the failure is loud: heater pinned at full output, coolant temperature stalled under target, the pack charging slowly, the driver fighting a loop that never catches up. Guess wrong the other way and it fails quietly, in the paperwork: a 12 kg unit where a smaller one would do, cable and fuses sized for capacity the duty cycle never calls on.

So send us the four numbers. Pack capacity, coldest realistic soak, departure window, cabin duty. We will show you the division before we quote a model, and if 15 kW does your job, 15 kW is what we will recommend. Questions we hear on this topic

Can two smaller heaters replace one 35 kW unit?

Yes. Paralleling standard units is normal practice on large vehicles, and it is how our mining-truck installations reach 210 kW. It also gives partial redundancy: one unit down is a bad day, not a parked truck.

Does a bigger heater drain the battery faster?

It drains it sooner, not more. The energy needed to warm a pack is fixed by mass and temperature lift. A larger heater delivers that energy in less time and usually wastes less of it to the surroundings on the way.

What decides the final size if battery and cabin need different power?

The larger of the two, checked against the departure window. In practice the battery warm-up case sizes most heavy trucks, and the cabin rides along on the same loop.

Nobody calls us because a heater failed its datasheet. They call in February, when a truck that passed every incoming inspection throwing faults on cold mornings — and the paperwork has a line that says IP67, another that says EMC passed, and both are technically true.

That gap, bench versus winter, is the subject here. We ship IP67 heaters across our A, E, H and Q series, and we would rather explain where the rating stops.

What the IP67 test actually puts a heater through

It comes from IEC 60529. The 6 means dust-tight. The 7 is an immersion test, and an oddly gentle one: clean water, one metre deep, thirty minutes, static. No jet, no spray. The unit is new, connectors mated as specified.

Passing it is worth having. Road spray, slush and standing water are its daily diet under a vehicle floor. Do not read more into the two digits than the test put there.

Where the rating stops: wash bays, birthdays, open connectors

Pressure washing kills more heaters than deep water. A wash bay is not an immersion tank: a pressure jet at half a metre drives water at the seal faces, and that is IP69K territory, a different rating altogether. Before fixing its position, find out how the depot washes the truck, and how close it gets.

Age is the second gap: the rating describes seals that have never been heat-cycled. Give that connector five winters of −40°C starts, hot coolant, chassis vibration and one hard pull on the harness, and it is not the seal that sat in the tank. What survives to year five comes down to seal compound and connector design, and none of that is on the datasheet.

The tested state matters. IP67 assumes the connectors are mated. A spare heater is not mated. Neither is a unit bolted down, connector open, through a week of vehicle build. We have taken that call: water inside a heater never plugged in, blamed on a seal it never got the chance to use.

And the rating faces outward only, judging the enclosure against dust and water from outside. Coolant lives behind the wall — casting porosity, hose joints, a freeze in a parked truck — and fails on its own schedule.

EMC cuts both ways, and the bench is not your truck

A high-voltage PTC heater is a multi-kilowatt switched load, and EMC testing is a bench exercise: in our case, ISO 11452 Class 3 levels on a defined harness, ground plane and load. Controlled and repeatable, nothing like a truck.

Your harness is longer, runs against a chassis rather than a ground plane, and the low-voltage ground lands where the body shop put it. Any of that moves the result, which is why the field symptom is so recognisable: CAN errors only on cold mornings, only at full heater power, gone by the workshop. The heater passed its bench test. The installation is failing one the bench never set.

Traffic runs the other way: load dumps, cranking transients, a noisy neighbour on the same supply all travel into the heater's electronics too. Read past the EMC line to the protection list — it is what stands between a transient and a dead unit: overvoltage, undervoltage, overcurrent, short circuit and overheating protection, plus a high-voltage interlock.

One number we would compare first

Ask where field life hides on a datasheet and we point at insulation resistance. Ours is specified at 100 MΩ or more for the high-voltage section, measured at DC 1000 V. Coolant does not stay insulating over years of service, and seals breathe a little; that figure is the margin absorbing all of it, slowly, not suddenly.

Compare that line, then ask the awkward follow-up: is it a design target on paper, or a tested value on production units? The answers sound alike in a sales email; on year four of a fleet they are not the same thing.

Five questions, and what the answers tell you

Ask these of any supplier, us included. None needs a lab, and the answers separate a rating earned on your configuration from one borrowed off a family datasheet.

  • That test report — is it for this exact configuration, connectors included, or a family report from a different model?
  • In what mated state was the IP test run, and what does the rating assume about the harness side?
  • Has it run at vehicle level, full power, on the production harness, with CAN error frames logged during the run?
  • The insulation resistance figure: what is it, and is it verified on every unit or sampled?
  • If we suspect water ingress in service, what does the warranty process need from us?

A supplier who has done the work answers in one email. A supplier who has not sends the datasheet again.

Questions we hear on this topic

Is IP67 enough for underbody mounting?

For spray, slush and standing water, yes, with connectors mated correctly and the wash routine keeping pressure jets at a distance. The rating alone is not the whole answer; the installation is the other half.

Can a heater pass EMC testing and still upset the vehicle network?

Yes. Bench compliance is measured in a defined setup; long harnesses, shared grounds and routing choices can create problems the bench never saw. That is why vehicle-level running at full power belongs in validation.

Does IP67 say anything about coolant leaks?

Nothing at all. It rates the enclosure against dust and water from outside. Internal coolant integrity is a separate design and test question.

"But the stack makes waste heat. Why are we buying a cabin heater at all?" That turns up at the first meeting of nearly every fuel cell bus project we talk to. Fair question. It survives until the first cold start, and not much longer.

Our answer never changes: specify a PTC coolant heater in the 20 kW class from the start, and treat stack waste heat as an offset, never as the heating system. Fleets running hydrogen buses through northern winters settled this years ago. The Beijing Winter Olympics fleets were the public example: waste-heat recovery plus an electric heater on every bus.

The stack starts the morning as a heating load

Begin with temperature. A diesel engine is a furnace that also turns wheels; its coolant comes off hot. A PEM fuel cell is efficient — the entire point of it — and its coolant runs cooler. Then the cold start: the stack is a cold mass, needing warming itself before it can work hard. For those first minutes it is not a heat source. It is one more load in the queue.

The bus has sat outside all night. The driver does his checks in the dark, windscreen opaque, first passengers waiting. The stack wants heat. The glass wants heat. The cabin has not warmed a degree. That is the moment the heating system was bought for. Not noon. Not cruise. Then.

Cruise is the easy part of the route

Published studies of fuel cell buses on winter cycles around −7°C show a consistent pattern: at steady cruise, stack waste heat covers part of the cabin load; drop into the low-load segments and an electric auxiliary heater carries the rest — most of it.

City duty is mostly low-load segments. Idling at a terminus. Crawling between stops. Doors opening at every stop while the stack sips hydrogen, makes little heat, and twenty passengers expect a warm cabin. Size it for cruise and it fails at the terminus daily, in front of the people it was bought for. Size it honestly and you have specified a PTC heater.

The water reaches projects late

One detail arrives late in these projects: the water. Fuel cell coolant is not ordinary coolant. The stack loop must stay at low electrical conductivity, so it runs deionised water with an ion exchanger, and anything that leaches ions into it starts a warranty argument. So a PTC heater rarely sits inside the stack loop. Usually the heater gets its own loop, or couples through a heat exchanger.

Which raises decisions teams meet too late: which loop the heater belongs to, who controls it, what happens to cabin heat if the stack faults. Decide it on paper. Deciding it during winter validation costs a season.

The load sum leaves a 20 kW hole

Do the arithmetic for a 12-metre city bus in northern service, item by item. First, the steady drain: heat leaking through panels and glass, a load that never stops. Second, the doors: every stop dumps warmed air on the pavement and pulls cold air in, every few hundred metres. Third, defrost: brief, front-loaded, not negotiable — the bus does not move until the driver can see. Add the three and the total lands in the 15–25 kW band.

Waste heat, when available, offsets part of that. What remains, on the worst morning not the average one, is a heater in the 20 kW class. That is why the figure recurs. It is not fashion. It is the size of the hole the stack leaves. Our E Series sits there, 20–24 kW at 400–800 V; larger cabins and colder routes step up a series, into Q.

What we would specify

For a 12-metre fuel cell city bus in cold-climate service: a PTC coolant heater in the 20 kW class on its own controlled loop, waste-heat recovery plumbed to offset it, CAN control so the energy manager can ration heat against hydrogen and route, and defrost-first priority after departure. Then validate on the coldest route, at the coldest hour, fully loaded. Averages heat nobody.

Questions we hear on this topic

So on a good day, could the stack heat the bus by itself?

On a mild day it can get close, and the heater may barely run. What it cannot cover is the gaps: cold starts, when the stack is warming itself, and low-load stretches, when there is little heat to recover. Those gaps are exactly when the cabin load bites. So the heater stays on the specification. Fitted, plumbed, mostly idle on the good days.

Why not just bolt on a diesel parking heater and be done with it?

Early fleets tried that, and thermally it works fine. The problem is the story: a bus sold to a city as zero-emission, burning diesel at the kerb, is a hard sell, and several zones restrict it outright. Electric heat keeps the emissions claim true. For most of these projects, that claim is the reason the bus exists.

Fine. But doesn't a 20 kW heater eat the range?

It draws real energy, out of the hydrogen or the buffer battery. We will not pretend it is free. But set it against the alternative: a bus that cannot clear its windscreen does not enter service at all. Precondition at the depot. Blend waste heat whenever the stack offers it. Let the energy manager ration the rest. The cost stays honest, and the bus works.

Start with the failure, because that is where heating systems cost money. A heater quits in January and the vehicle stays in the yard; nobody cares which unit was cheaper on the purchase order. Both architectures suffer it, and neither camp's brochure will tell you otherwise. We manufacture coolant heaters, so discount what follows for that bias — then check the arithmetic anyway, because the quote is the smallest part of this one.

On price, air wins, and it is not close

An air PTC heater is a heating element, a fan and a housing — not much to it, and that is a compliment. A coolant heater is a pressure-rated liquid device: high-voltage connectors, seals, a control board, and it expects a pump and plumbing around it. Unit for unit air is cheaper to buy and to ship; any supplier arguing otherwise is selling something. Air has that line.

Installation turns on one question: is there a loop?

Air heat needs ducting and high-voltage wiring run to where the people sit. Coolant heat needs hoses, fittings, a bleed procedure and a place in the liquid loop. Quotes price the two lists as equivalent work. On a modern commercial EV they are not: the battery is liquid-cooled, so the loop is already there — circuit routed, pump fitted, coolant already on the service schedule. Tapping a heater into that circuit is incremental, not a new system.

Reverse the vehicle and the answer reverses. On an older or air-cooled platform with no loop at all, a coolant heater drags a whole circuit in behind it — pump, hoses, reservoir, the lot — and the air heater wins installation outright. We would tell the buyer so.

The battery is the argument

An air heater heats air. It cannot warm a liquid-cooled battery. That is not a limitation to engineer around; it is what the device is. In a cold climate the battery needs heat regardless — for charge acceptance, for power, for longevity. The northern fleet that bought the cheaper heater then buys battery heating as a second, separate system: a second control integration, and a second way for the vehicle to end up in the yard.

One coolant heater does both jobs through one loop and a set of valves. It warms the pack before departure, then serves the cabin on the road. That is why coolant heaters dominate commercial EVs despite the higher unit price — not that the heater is cheaper, but that the second heater never gets bought.

Energy, honestly, is a draw

Resistive heating turns electricity into heat at roughly one to one, whichever box it happens in. Twenty kilowatts for one hour is twenty kilowatt-hours, out of either box. Anyone claiming a dramatic efficiency gap between the two is marketing at you.

The differences sit at the edges. A coolant system can precondition pack and cabin on depot power before departure, spending grid energy instead of battery energy, and on the road it can blend motor waste heat into the same loop. An air heater warms a cabin quickly and hands the heat back as quickly when the doors open and the fan stops — on a city bus duty cycle you can see it in the energy log. A few percent. Not a revolution.

What the fleet lives with afterwards

Maintenance, first. A coolant heater rides on a circuit the fleet already maintains; coolant changes, hose inspections and pump life are known quantities. Air heater elements live in dusty ducts and take a thermal shock at every cold start. Neither is maintenance-free, whatever the brochures say.

When a heater does fail in January, the vehicle is out of service under both architectures; what differs is what happens next. One heating system type across the fleet means one spare on the shelf and one diagnostic routine every technician knows. Fleets that split architectures to save on the purchase price hand the saving back in spares and training within a few winters.

Drivers, last, because no spreadsheet carries them. A cab that warms slowly on a January morning produces complaints, workarounds, and eventually a request to idle something that should not be idling. A vehicle that preconditioned on the plug leaves warm and generates no emails.

Where we land

Choose air when the vehicle has no liquid loop, the climate is mild, the job is cabin-only, or the project is a retrofit where plumbing is impractical. Small vans and spot heating for a sleeper cab are fair examples, and on those vehicles our own product is the wrong buy. Choose coolant when the battery is liquid-cooled, winters are real, or the vehicle has to precondition on shore power — which covers most commercial EV programmes we see. Run your duty cycle through the costs above before either quote on price.

Questions we hear on this topic

Is a coolant heater worth it on a small electric van?

Depends on the battery and the winters. Liquid-cooled pack, cold climate: usually yes, because the battery-heating job is already there waiting. Air-cooled pack, southern duty: the air heater is the rational buy, and we would say so.

Does a coolant heater warm the cabin more slowly?

Slightly, on a fully cold start — the loop has to come up to temperature first. The difference is minutes, and preconditioning while plugged in removes it entirely.

Should we compare either option against a heat pump?

On any new platform, yes. Heat pumps cut heating energy in moderate cold and lose ground in deep cold, which is why so many vehicles pair one with a PTC heater. We compared the two in our heat pump article.

Two in the morning at a swap station, January. A driver on the second night shift of his week rolls in low; the gantry does its work; about five minutes later he pulls out with a full pack. As logistics, hard to fault. Then he turns the cab heater on, watches the windscreen mist up, and learns what those five minutes did not buy him.

Swapping removes the charging wait, not the temperature. We say this to fleet engineers a lot — usually in a swap project's first winter, after the specification is signed. The thermal problem has not gone anywhere; it has moved house, part into the station, part still riding on the truck. The part on the truck gets forgotten.

The pack's second home is a rack

A plug-in truck's pack sleeps on the vehicle, charging slowly overnight. A swap pack lives harder. Charged hard at the station, swapped in, worked a shift, swapped out, charged hard again. Standardised swap packs for heavy trucks now run past 400 kWh, with current designs at 432 and 513 kWh. Charge a mass that size at commercial speed and the heat goes somewhere: onto the station's problem list.

So does the cold.

A pack sitting in an unheated rack through a winter night charges slowly, and ages while it does it. Cold cells accept charge reluctantly; forcing it costs cycle life. So the station inherits the truck's old job: packs held in a ready temperature window, warmed ahead of the morning peak, cooled through the afternoon charging waves. That capacity belongs in the station design documents, sized for the busiest hour — averages are how a station ends up with a queue of cold packs at six in the morning.

The driver still works in January

The cabin did not move into the rack. The driver needs heat and a clear windscreen at −20°C on every shift, and a pack arriving at station temperature does nothing for either. Cabin heating on a swap truck is the same unglamorous job as on any heavy EV: a high-voltage coolant heater on the vehicle's own loop — in our line the Q Series, 15–35 kW across 400–1100 V.

The pack drifts too. It leaves the station inside its window, then works a long cold haul. Temperature falls, available power falls with it, and the truck needs enough on-board heating to hold the pack in a working band until the next swap. Whoever specced the vehicle assuming the station handles everything thermal finds out on the first January night route — we would rather they found out on paper.

The couplings take a daily beating

A plug-in truck's coolant loop connects to nothing; it is sealed for life, more or less. A swap pack's fluid couplings, where the design uses them, are mated and unmated every working day — in grit, in frost, in a hurry. Every cycle wears seals, tests the self-sealing valves, invites air in. An air lock that is a once-in-a-decade event on a sealed truck becomes routine statistics on a swap fleet.

Anyone specifying swap vehicles needs three habits. Get the coupling cycle rating in writing. Design the fill and bleed procedure for the station, not the workshop. Log coolant condition as a station KPI. Thermal reliability on a swap network is mostly interface discipline; the chemistry gets blamed anyway.

Five minutes in, two hours out

Run the January scenario. A pack goes in at −15°C because the station held it cold overnight to save energy. The truck pulls away with a full charge it cannot fully use: discharge power limited, regenerative braking limited, no fast top-up accepted until the pack warms through. The swap took five minutes. The penalty lasts about two hours.

Warming it at the station, on grid power, before the swap costs less and none of the truck's working day. Which is why the swap agreement needs a thermal clause: pack temperature at handover, as a stated number, owned by the station operator. Early swap contracts tend to leave that line out. Its absence is discovered the expensive way.

What we would put in the specification

If a fleet sent us a swap specification tomorrow, these are the five lines we would check first:

  • Station conditioning capacity sized for the peak swap hour, heating and cooling.
  • A handover temperature window for packs, written into the operating agreement.
  • Truck-side coolant heater sized for cabin, defrost and pack temperature holding, independent of the station.
  • Coolant coupling cycle ratings and a station-level bleed and fill procedure.
  • Temperature logging per pack, per cycle, reviewed like any other fleet KPI.

Swapping is a strong answer to charging time. It is not an answer to temperature, and the projects that treat it as one end up retrofitting heaters in the second winter.

Questions we hear on this topic

Do swap trucks need smaller on-board heaters?

No. The cabin and defrost loads are unchanged, and pack temperature holding on route still needs heat. The station takes over charging-related conditioning, not the truck's winter.

Can station conditioning replace on-board battery heating completely?

It replaces pre-departure warming and charging conditioning. It cannot hold a pack's temperature through a long cold shift. Both halves of the system earn their place.

Who should pay for preconditioning energy at the station?

That is a commercial question, but it has to be asked in the contract, alongside the handover temperature. Energy spent warming packs is small next to the cycle life and downtime it protects.

Ask what the quoted configuration includes

For a vehicle that requires preconditioning, it leaves a question to resolve: which heater, control arrangement and installation items are included? We would write those items into the comparison rather than infer them from a cooling rating.

The published 5 kW BTMS product page describes the LDC560HA configuration with 6 kW heating. That does not convert every 5 kW unit in the range into the same configuration. Use the model and revision on the actual quotation.

A 12 kW cooling label does not override its electrical limit. The 400–860 V models merit further electrical review, but that range alone does not prove the complete vehicle match.

Keep cooling, heating and electrical input separate

A 5 kW cooling rating describes a heat-removal duty under its stated conditions. It is not a claim that electrical input is 5 kW. Likewise, a heating-capacity entry and a cooling-capacity entry are different duties, not values to add into a single universal system rating.

For an illustration, delivering a constant 6 kW of heat for half an hour transfers 3 kWh of thermal energy. That calculation says nothing by itself about how quickly a specific battery warms. Pack thermal mass, initial temperature, heat losses, coolant distribution and the actual available heater output still determine the response. It is not an EVLINK warm-up test.

For charging and driving, distinguish the peak from the duration and repetition of the load. For heating, state the starting condition, target and time available. If those data are unknown, the sensible next step is a requirements review, not a confident recommendation of 12 kW.

We would not replace a high-voltage coolant heater just because the vehicle sends an enable request and the coolant stays cold. Before calling the heater faulty, establish what that particular unit is meant to accept. A power connection, a control request and useful heat in the coolant are different observations.

Our B and E Series make the distinction concrete. Both appear in the EVLINK specification summary at 20–24 kW and 400–800 V. B Series is listed with CAN and an enable interface. E Series uses thermostat control, without CAN or an external control interface. Choosing a unit from the power and voltage columns alone can therefore leave the vehicle expecting a conversation the heater was never designed to have.

The same kilowatts do not mean the same start command

Suppose a project requires the vehicle controller to request heating and read a defined status over CAN. B Series is a candidate to investigate; the E Series entry does not meet that communication requirement. That is a selection mismatch to resolve before commissioning, not evidence that an E Series unit has a CAN fault.

The exact B variant still needs its approved interface document. “CAN plus enable” does not provide a pinout, message identifier, wake-up sequence or diagnostic definition. We would not fill those gaps from another heater's manual. Similar power ratings do not make the wiring or software interchangeable.

What a 22 kW calculation can tell you

Consider a deliberately simplified electrical example: a heater taking 22 kW from a 600 V DC source. Dividing power by voltage gives 22,000 / 600, or approximately 36.7 A. That is an arithmetic reference for those assumed conditions, not a measured current from an EVLINK heater.

It does not establish that the control electronics have their required supply, that a command was accepted or that the heater delivers 22 kW at every point in its voltage range. It is not a cable or fuse recommendation either. Those decisions need the selected unit's actual limits and the vehicle electrical design.

If the available evidence consists only of a nominal battery voltage and an enable request in the VCU log, the diagnosis is still open. A calculation cannot supply the missing observations.

Start with the event that failed

For an intermittent no-start, a voltage reading taken several minutes later is a weak substitute for a record of the attempted start. We would ask the responsible engineer to capture the relevant control-supply condition at the approved observation point, together with the documented wake-up or enable input and any feedback the variant actually provides.

Keep the timing relationship. Did the unit reach its defined ready state before a heating request arrived? Did a supply or vehicle-state change coincide with the failed attempt? These are questions for the evidence package. They are not universal timing rules; the released interface specification determines the required sequence.

Do not invent a ready-state signal if the model has none. For a thermostat-controlled unit, follow its own control architecture. An empty CAN trace cannot prove that a non-CAN heater is defective.

Awake is not the same as allowed to heat

A controller may be awake while heating remains inhibited. The particular heater and vehicle documentation determines which conditions matter and which states are observable. Use those definitions to interpret an alarm or a refusal to heat, rather than treating every inhibit as an internal hardware failure.

Preserve the original fault information before a reset. If a restart makes the symptom disappear, retain that result alongside the failed attempt; do not rewrite the history as “no fault found”. A controlled comparison between successful and unsuccessful attempts can be more useful than another catalogue page.

Cold coolant does not identify an electrical cause

Even when electrical input is established, the thermal side remains a separate question. Coolant flow, starting temperature, circuit mass, sensor positions and the observation period affect what a temperature trace means. Use the existing HVCH power-verification guide to distinguish a requested output, electrical input and delivered heat.

A no-start investigation should stay focused. First establish whether the documented start and heating conditions were met. If the unit is taking power but the coolant response is unexpected, move to the thermal investigation with those electrical observations attached. Do not keep changing communication settings to solve an unverified hydraulic problem.

High-voltage access and measurements require qualified personnel and the vehicle's approved isolation and service procedures. Do not bridge an interlock, force a power stage on, apply an assumed test voltage or use an unverified connector pin assignment. The purpose of the review is to identify the next justified inspection, not to improvise a live test.

A useful evidence package identifies the heater part and software version, the interface revision, the requested operation, the observed supply and states, and the fault information.
