<p>Two in the morning at a swap station, January. A driver on the second night shift of his week rolls in low; the gantry does its work; about five minutes later he pulls out with a full pack. As logistics, hard to fault. Then he turns the cab heater on, watches the windscreen mist up, and learns what those five minutes did not buy him.</p>

<p>Swapping removes the charging wait, not the temperature. We say this to fleet engineers a lot — usually in a swap project's first winter, after the specification is signed. The thermal problem has not gone anywhere; it has moved house, part into the station, part still riding on the truck. The part on the truck gets forgotten.</p>

<h2>The pack's second home is a rack</h2>

<p>A plug-in truck's pack sleeps on the vehicle, charging slowly overnight. A swap pack lives harder. Charged hard at the station, swapped in, worked a shift, swapped out, charged hard again. Standardised swap packs for heavy trucks now run past 400 kWh, with current designs at 432 and 513 kWh. Charge a mass that size at commercial speed and the heat goes somewhere: onto the station's problem list.</p>

<p>So does the cold.</p>

<p>A pack sitting in an unheated rack through a winter night charges slowly, and ages while it does it. Cold cells accept charge reluctantly; forcing it costs cycle life. So the station inherits the truck's old job: packs held in a ready temperature window, warmed ahead of the morning peak, cooled through the afternoon charging waves. That capacity belongs in the station design documents, sized for the busiest hour — averages are how a station ends up with a queue of cold packs at six in the morning.</p>

<h2>The driver still works in January</h2>

<p>The cabin did not move into the rack. The driver needs heat and a clear windscreen at −20°C on every shift, and a pack arriving at station temperature does nothing for either. Cabin heating on a swap truck is the same unglamorous job as on any heavy EV: a high-voltage coolant heater on the vehicle's own loop — in our line the Q Series, 15–35 kW across 400–1100 V.</p>

<p>The pack drifts too. It leaves the station inside its window, then works a long cold haul. Temperature falls, available power falls with it, and the truck needs enough on-board heating to hold the pack in a working band until the next swap. Whoever specced the vehicle assuming the station handles everything thermal finds out on the first January night route — we would rather they found out on paper.</p>

<h2>The couplings take a daily beating</h2>

<p>A plug-in truck's coolant loop connects to nothing; it is sealed for life, more or less. A swap pack's fluid couplings, where the design uses them, are mated and unmated every working day — in grit, in frost, in a hurry. Every cycle wears seals, tests the self-sealing valves, invites air in. An air lock that is a once-in-a-decade event on a sealed truck becomes routine statistics on a swap fleet.</p>

<p>Anyone specifying swap vehicles needs three habits. Get the coupling cycle rating in writing. Design the fill and bleed procedure for the station, not the workshop. Log coolant condition as a station KPI. Thermal reliability on a swap network is mostly interface discipline; the chemistry gets blamed anyway.</p>

<h2>Five minutes in, two hours out</h2>

<p>Run the January scenario. A pack goes in at −15°C because the station held it cold overnight to save energy. The truck pulls away with a full charge it cannot fully use: discharge power limited, regenerative braking limited, no fast top-up accepted until the pack warms through. The swap took five minutes. The penalty lasts about two hours.</p>

<p>Warming it at the station, on grid power, before the swap costs less and none of the truck's working day. Which is why the swap agreement needs a thermal clause: pack temperature at handover, as a stated number, owned by the station operator. Early swap contracts tend to leave that line out. Its absence is discovered the expensive way.</p>

<h2>What we would put in the specification</h2>

<p>If a fleet sent us a swap specification tomorrow, these are the five lines we would check first:</p>

<ul>
<li>Station conditioning capacity sized for the peak swap hour, heating and cooling.</li>
<li>A handover temperature window for packs, written into the operating agreement.</li>
<li>Truck-side coolant heater sized for cabin, defrost and pack temperature holding, independent of the station.</li>
<li>Coolant coupling cycle ratings and a station-level bleed and fill procedure.</li>
<li>Temperature logging per pack, per cycle, reviewed like any other fleet KPI.</li>
</ul>

<p>Swapping is a strong answer to charging time. It is not an answer to temperature, and the projects that treat it as one end up retrofitting heaters in the second winter.</p>

<h2>Questions we hear on this topic</h2>

<h3>Do swap trucks need smaller on-board heaters?</h3>
<p>No. The cabin and defrost loads are unchanged, and pack temperature holding on route still needs heat. The station takes over charging-related conditioning, not the truck's winter.</p>

<h3>Can station conditioning replace on-board battery heating completely?</h3>
<p>It replaces pre-departure warming and charging conditioning. It cannot hold a pack's temperature through a long cold shift. Both halves of the system earn their place.</p>

<h3>Who should pay for preconditioning energy at the station?</h3>
<p>That is a commercial question, but it has to be asked in the contract, alongside the handover temperature. Energy spent warming packs is small next to the cycle life and downtime it protects.</p>
