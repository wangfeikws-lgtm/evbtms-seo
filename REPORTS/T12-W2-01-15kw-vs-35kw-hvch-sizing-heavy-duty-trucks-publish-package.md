<p>The question lands in our inbox almost word for word: "Your Q Series runs from 15 to 35 kW. Which one do we need?" The honest answer frustrates people. It depends on three numbers the buyer usually has not calculated yet: how much battery mass has to be warmed, how cold it soaks overnight, and how many minutes the truck can spend plugged in before it has to leave.</p>

<p>Kilowatts are not a trim level. A 35 kW unit is not a better 15 kW unit. It is a different tool, and picking between them is arithmetic, not taste.</p>

<h2>One heater, three different jobs</h2>

<p>A coolant heater on a heavy truck does three jobs that scale differently. Warming the battery is an energy problem: a fixed amount of heat has to go into a large mass, and power only decides how long it takes. Holding cabin temperature at cruise is a power problem: heat leaks out through glass and panels at a steady rate and the heater has to match it. Defrost is a peak problem: a cold-soaked windscreen at departure needs a short, hard burst. Size for the wrong job and the truck tells you about it all winter.</p>

<h2>Run the battery arithmetic first</h2>

<p>Take a long-haul tractor with a pack in the 300–350 kWh class. The pack alone weighs roughly two tonnes. Cell specific heat sits near 1 kJ per kilogram per degree, so lifting that pack by 20°C takes about 40 megajoules of heat, call it 11 kWh, before the coolant, housings and pipework take their share.</p>

<p>Now the sizing question answers itself. A 15 kW heater needs around 45 minutes of flat-out work just for the pack, longer once losses are counted. A 35 kW heater does the same job in under 20 minutes. If the truck cold-soaks at −25°C and leaves 30 minutes after the driver arrives, 15 kW was never a candidate, no matter what the quote said. If the same truck parks indoors and preconditions for two hours on depot power, 35 kW is money spent heating a building.</p>

<h2>Then check the cabin and the glass</h2>

<p>A truck cab is a small space, and holding it at temperature in deep cold typically takes a few kilowatts once it is warm. The expensive moments are the first ten minutes: cold seats, cold panels, and a windscreen that must clear before the vehicle moves. City buses sit at the other extreme. A 12-metre cabin with doors opening every few hundred metres can want two to three times the heater capacity of a truck cab, which is why bus projects land on the upper half of the range so often.</p>

<p>This is also where the 210 kW figure in our material comes from, because people ask. On mining trucks we parallel six 35 kW units for a combined 210 kW per vehicle. Nothing exotic is happening there. The heat load is simply that large, and parallel standard units beat one bespoke giant on serviceability every time.</p>

<h2>When 15 kW is the right answer</h2>

<p>Fifteen kilowatts is plenty when the climate is moderate, the pack is modest, and the schedule allows a real preconditioning window on shore power. Regional delivery trucks that return to a depot every night are the classic case. The heater works steadily, the truck leaves warm, and the smaller unit's lower current draw keeps harness and fuse sizing simple. At 400 V, even 15 kW pulls close to 38 A. This is not a trivial circuit.</p>

<h2>When 35 kW earns its place</h2>

<p>Step up when any of these show up in the duty cycle: cold soaks below −20°C, departure windows under half an hour, packs above 300 kWh, or a vehicle that must heat while it works rather than while it charges. Mining and construction trucks qualify on every count. So do northern line-haul fleets whose trucks sleep outside.</p>

<p>At 800 V a 35 kW unit draws about 44 A; at 400 V it draws about 88 A. That difference is why the Q Series spans 400–1100 V. On an 870 V platform, and we have shipped into DC870V truck programmes since 2021, the high-power end of the range stops being a packaging fight.</p>

<h2>The cost of guessing wrong, both ways</h2>

<p>Undersizing fails loudly. The heater pins at 100% output, coolant temperature stalls below target, the pack accepts charge slowly, and the driver runs the cab heater against a loop that never catches up. Oversizing fails quietly, on the invoice and in the harness: heavier cable, bigger fuses, a 12 kg unit where a smaller one would do, and purchase cost spent on capacity the duty cycle never calls.</p>

<p>Our recommendation has not changed in years. Send us the pack capacity, the coldest realistic soak, the departure window and the cabin duty, and we will show you the arithmetic before we quote a model. If 15 kW does your job, we would rather sell you 15 kW.</p>

<h2>Questions we hear on this topic</h2>

<h3>Can two smaller heaters replace one 35 kW unit?</h3>
<p>Yes. Paralleling standard units is normal practice on large vehicles, and it is how our mining-truck installations reach 210 kW. It also gives partial redundancy: one unit down is a bad day, not a parked truck.</p>

<h3>Does a bigger heater drain the battery faster?</h3>
<p>It drains it sooner, not more. The energy needed to warm a pack is fixed by mass and temperature lift. A larger heater delivers that energy in less time and usually wastes less of it to the surroundings on the way.</p>

<h3>What decides the final size if battery and cabin need different power?</h3>
<p>The larger of the two, checked against the departure window. In practice the battery warm-up case sizes most heavy trucks, and the cabin rides along on the same loop.</p>
