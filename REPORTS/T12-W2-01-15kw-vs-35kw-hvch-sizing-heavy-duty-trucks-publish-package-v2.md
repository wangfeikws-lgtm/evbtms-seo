<p>The email usually shows up a few days after the quote goes out. <p>We do not answer with a model number. We ask three questions back. How heavy is the pack? How cold does the truck sit overnight? And once the driver shows up, how many minutes before that truck has to move?</p>

<p>Short answer, before the working: the departure window decides it more often than the truck does. A 35 kW unit is not an upgraded 15 kW unit. It is the same job done against a shorter clock.</p>

<h2>Do the pack sum first</h2>

<p>Take a long-haul tractor, pack in the 300–350 kWh class. Forget the heater for a moment and weigh the battery. A pack that size comes in at roughly two tonnes.</p>

<p>Now the heat. Cell specific heat sits near 1 kJ per kilogram per degree. Two thousand kilograms, lifted 20°C, at 1 kJ a kilo-degree: that is 40,000 kJ, or 40 megajoules — about 11 kWh of heat that has to physically be inside those cells. The coolant, housings and pipework take their cut on the way in, so treat 11 kWh as the floor, not the total.</p>

<p>Last step, divide by power. Hand that 11 kWh to a 15 kW heater and it needs around 45 minutes running flat out, longer once the losses above go in. Hand it to a 35 kW heater and the same lift is done in under 20 minutes.</p>

<p>That one division settles most of these enquiries.</p>

<p>Picture the truck cold-soaked at −25°C, driver on site, wheels turning 30 minutes later. The 15 kW unit never had a chance. Now park the identical truck indoors, on depot power with two hours to precondition. The maths flips completely: the extra capacity is money spent warming a building. Same truck, same pack, opposite answer. This is why we refuse to pick from the rating plate alone.</p>

<h2>The cab rarely sizes the heater. The glass sometimes does.</h2>

<p>Once everything is warm, holding a truck cab at temperature in deep cold only takes a few kilowatts. The cab is a small box. What costs power is the first ten minutes: cold seats, cold panels, and a windscreen that has to be clear before the wheels move. Defrost is a short, hard burst, landing at exactly the moment the battery is also asking for everything the heater has.</p>

<p>Buses break this pattern, and bus buyers should not borrow truck numbers. A 12-metre city bus cabin, doors opening every few hundred metres, can call for two to three times the heater capacity of a truck cab. We went through the cold-end planning side of that in our notes on <a href="https://www.evptc.com/news/electric-bus-battery-preheating-at-30-c-five-planning-mistakes-422919.html">electric bus battery preheating at −30°C</a>.</p>

<h2>Then ask what the wiring thinks</h2>

<p>Power ratings look clean on paper. Current is where they get physical. A 15 kW heater at 400 V pulls close to 38 A. That is already not a trivial circuit, and it is the small unit.</p>

<p>The 35 kW unit shows the split clearly. At 800 V it draws about 44 A, manageable. At 400 V the same unit wants about 88 A, which means heavier cable, bigger fuses, and a harness conversation nobody enjoys late in a programme. This is the whole reason the Q Series spans 400–1100 V. On an 870 V platform the big unit stops being a packaging fight, and we have been shipping into DC870V truck programmes since 2021.</p>

<p>Mining trucks take the logic one step further. Nobody fits one enormous heater. Our installations parallel six 35 kW units for a combined 210 kW per vehicle, because the heat load genuinely is that large and a fitter can swap one standard unit at the roadside. Try doing that with a single bespoke giant.</p>

<h2>Where we land, case by case</h2>

<p>Fifteen kilowatts, when the climate is moderate, the pack is modest, and the truck comes home every night to a depot where it can precondition properly on shore power. Regional delivery work is the classic case, and we will happily sell the smaller unit there. Oversizing it helps no one.</p>

<p>Thirty-five, when any of these turn up: cold soaks below −20°C, a departure window under half an hour, a pack over 300 kWh, or a vehicle that has to heat while it works instead of while it charges. Mining and construction trucks tick every box on that list. Northern line-haul fleets sleeping outdoors tick most of them.</p>

<p>Guess wrong one way and the failure is loud: heater pinned at full output, coolant temperature stalled under target, the pack charging slowly, the driver fighting a loop that never catches up. Guess wrong the other way and it fails quietly, in the paperwork: a 12 kg unit where a smaller one would do, cable and fuses sized for capacity the duty cycle never calls on.</p>

<h2>Questions we hear on this topic</h2>

<h3>Can two smaller heaters replace one 35 kW unit?</h3>
<p>Yes. Paralleling standard units is normal practice on large vehicles, and it is how our mining-truck installations reach 210 kW. It also gives partial redundancy: one unit down is a bad day, not a parked truck.</p>

<h3>Does a bigger heater drain the battery faster?</h3>
<p>It drains it sooner, not more. The energy needed to warm a pack is fixed by mass and temperature lift. A larger heater delivers that energy in less time and usually wastes less of it to the surroundings on the way.</p>

<h3>What decides the final size if battery and cabin need different power?</h3>
<p>The larger of the two, checked against the departure window. In practice the battery warm-up case sizes most heavy trucks, and the cabin rides along on the same loop.</p>
